## Musical identity
This fictional guest is a co-composer with an intimate reed voice: able to propose a melodic sentence, a modal center, a breathing answer, or a change in the ensemble’s density. Their strongest proposed use is reflective discovery, quiet wonder, and night music. They can also contribute a restrained rhythmic phrase when the cue calls for movement.

The chair’s coherence comes from attentive phrasing and ensemble dialogue. Duduk, soprano clarinet, and bass clarinet require separate technical knowledge. Their inclusion in one studio chair does not make their embouchures, fingerings, ornaments, tuning practices, or cultural vocabularies interchangeable.

A good contribution has an identifiable beginning, destination, and release. Atmospheric sound supports that musical thought. The guest can also rest for an entire section—or an entire song.

## Technique, dynamics, and phrase construction
The following are proposed abilities of the fictional performer.

- **Onset:** distinguish a gently defined attack from a connected entrance. A quiet note must still establish its pitch and rhythmic intention. Avoid giving every note the same slow fade, which can blur the hook and delay answers.
- **Sustain:** shape toward a chosen phrase peak, then release energy. Quiet playing retains a stable center. Increasing intensity should involve phrasing and tone as well as level.
- **Release:** decide whether the note settles, tapers, or ends clearly before another player enters. Preserve the release in the performance; reverb should not supply the entire ending.
- **Breath:** mark a believable breath plan. For initial planning, consider short two-to-four-second answers and longer four-to-eight-second lines, with genuine gaps. These are arranging proposals, not physiological limits. Tempo, intensity, register, and actual player or sample response determine the final duration.
- **Vibrato:** begin with a stable center where appropriate; add a restrained, phrase-specific amount. Avoid identical vibrato depth and speed on every sustained note. Do not add modulation over baked-in vibrato without checking the result.
- **Ornament:** choose a small number of purposeful approaches, grace figures, or inflections after instrument-specific study. Do not import flute fluttering, whistle cuts, or generic “world wind” runs.
- **Rhythmic articulation:** make a short repeated figure intelligible through placement and release. Connected playing and separately tongued notes serve different musical jobs.

Duduk pedagogy by Shea “Sheram” A. J. Comfort describes a broad double reed held between the lips without tooth contact, a primarily octave-centered range with additional notes, and active tuning through reed, lip, and finger control. Its half-holing guidance emphasizes cleanly reaching the intended pitch. These details support separate duduk performance design; they do not establish the behavior of an untested UVI program. [Duduk.com lesson guide](https://www.duduk.com/duduk-faq/)

Clarinet preparation uses a different mouthpiece/reed relationship. Yamaha’s guide describes lower-lip contact with the reed and upper-teeth contact with the mouthpiece. Clarinet tonguing and pitch shaping need their own treatment. The proposed player must know that a softened duduk phrase cannot simply be transferred as identical clarinet control data. [Yamaha playing guide](https://www.yamaha.com/en/musical_instrument_guide/clarinet/play/)

## Sustained melody and drone are separate assignments
A long melodic note still belongs to a sentence: it has direction, an endpoint, and a relationship to the next phrase. A drone establishes a continuing tonal reference and needs its own tuning and dynamic purpose.

Circular breathing is an optional, documented drone context. It is not a default explanation for unlimited melodic endurance. If melody and drone occur simultaneously, assign a second player or declare a studio overdub. If this single guest changes instruments, allow a real transition or identify an edit.

A synthesized pad may provide an openly declared electronic foundation. It should not be described as a performed duduk drone without the corresponding instrument and evidence.

## Musical knowledge
The guest should understand tonal centers, interval relationships, modal collections, chord tones, suspensions, harmonic rhythm, and the difference between a structural pitch and its expressive approach. They should be able to discuss a phrase in scale degrees and rhythm with colleagues, then write down the agreed version.

For duduk, agree how flexible intonation meets fixed-pitch piano, Sunbird, mallets, or synths. Before using a sustained note, inspect every bass and chord change underneath it. A pedal that works at entry may become an unwanted clash later. Preserve the contour and essential pitches of approved melodies; if the instrument cannot deliver them credibly, choose another assignment.

The player can propose a modal color without claiming expertise in every Armenian tradition, makam, raga, or other system. Name the specific tradition when it matters, select a qualified contextual source, and avoid treating all modal music as one vocabulary.

Rhythmically, the guest should maintain pulse through long notes and rests, enter confidently after a hand-drum call, and place an answer within the ensemble’s agreed swing or subdivision. Afro-Caribbean interaction comes from learning the actual groove with the percussion and bass collaborators. Duduk ornament is not automatically a clave pattern.

## Writing, improvisation, and collaboration
The guest may originate a hook, a settling answer, a modal center, a counterline, or a formal suggestion. They should explain what the child is meant to recognize when the idea returns.

Exploration can include bounded improvisation over agreed harmony and duration. Once a direction is selected, write the principal notes, rhythm, breaths, dynamics, ornaments, and handoffs. Keep the approved hook stable at meaningful returns. Variation can change register, orchestration, articulation, or the answer without turning every recurrence into a new solo.

Useful habits include:

- Offer one complete sentence before proposing additional layers.
- Ask another musician to answer rather than filling the resulting gap.
- Let bass or percussion introduce a section when their idea carries its identity.
- Thin a former foreground part when another player takes over.
- Preserve alternate takes and the written decision that selects between them.
- Treat a complete rest as a valid contribution to the arrangement.

A full song needs defined contrasting sections and a recognizable return. Reflective music can use an opening, lyrical A, contrasting B, changed A, and coda. “Atmospheric” does not remove the need for form.

Before declaring a reed program ready:

1. Record bank/version, exact program path, native state, source identity, license, and recall evidence.
2. Map sounding pitches and MIDI limits. Check root/key, octave, tuning reference, usable central register, and every required accidental.
3. Compare soft, medium, and stronger notes for attack, tone, tuning, and unwanted layer changes.
4. Test separated and connected notes, repeated pitches, several interval sizes, sustain lengths, releases, and the intended breath gap.
5. Identify real legato, sampled ornaments, baked vibrato, release samples, and supported expression controls. Do not assume a particular CC mapping or keyswitch.
6. Check pitch inflections against sustained fixed-pitch partners. Record any retuning or transposition and its audible cost.
7. Compare dry and produced versions in mono and stereo, then in the intended ensemble and voice mix.

An extended keyboard mapping, long MIDI note, or smooth pitch bend is insufficient evidence of a believable reed performance.

## Tone and atmospheric production
Aim for an audible reed core with gentle edges and a clear phrase center. Preserve enough direct signal to distinguish articulation from the room around it.

Begin with the native source and balance. Audition any high-pass filter against the lowest used fundamental; avoid removing the body to make the part fit. Inspect low-mid overlap with guitar/EP and upper-mid competition with speech, then compare each change with bypass. Numerical frequency areas guide investigation; they do not diagnose an unheard sound.

Use restrained ambience to place the guest in the same musical world as the ensemble. A longer electronic tail may support a discovery passage, provided the foreground release and harmony remain readable. Avoid broad chorus, constant pitch drift, or saturation that obscures tuning and mono identity. Preserve dry, edited, and processed versions separately.

The lead should remain understandable at low playback level. A quieter accompaniment or a written rest may solve masking more effectively than further processing.

## Failure modes and acceptance questions
Reject or revise a contribution when it becomes a continuous pad with no phrase; when every note scoops or vibrates identically; when ornamental detail weakens the approved hook; when the drone fights changing harmony; or when an expressive reed is reduced to a generic sadness cue.

Before advancing a candidate, ask:

- Can a listener recognize the hook through its entrances, breaths, and returns?
- Does the selected program support the written action at the chosen register and intensity?
- Are pitch inflections intentional, with a stable destination?
- Can another player answer without competing lines or lingering tails?
- Are simultaneous parts and instrument changes physically explained?
- Does the location retain its own identity?
- Do speech, mono playback, repeated listening, and the child’s hardware preserve the intended result?

Human review must decide musical warmth, style, fatigue, and appropriateness. Machine measurements cannot answer those questions.
