# Auxiliary Latin-percussion performer — `aux_percussion`
**Draft revision:** 1 · **Date:** 2026-11-01<br>
**Status:** Proposed fictional performer capabilities and future arranging guidance. Full-song construction remains paused pending owner review.

## Musical identity
An attentive percussionist and co-composer who gives a song rhythmic detail, a recognizable answering gesture, or a clear sectional turn. The chair’s strongest contribution is knowing which surface, accent or silence makes the ensemble’s intention clearer.

Timbales are the principal specialty. Bongos, cowbell, güiro, claves and selected small auxiliaries are related capabilities requiring their own technique and sound choices. The performer can originate a rhythmic hook, suggest a formal break, lead a short passage, accompany another musician or rest for an entire cue.

This is a specialist guest alongside the existing Afro-Caribbean percussionist, traditional/world percussionist and acoustic drummer. A guest assignment needs a musical or physical reason. Four available percussion sounds do not call for four continuous parts.

## Proposed musical knowledge
The performer understands pulse, subdivision, meter and phrase position well enough to maintain time through silence and re-enter without needing every beat sounded.

Expected working abilities include:

- Distinguishing straight duple subdivision, triple subdivision and compound meter.
- Understanding the difference between a 3:2 rhythmic relationship and the direction of a two-bar clave.
- Recognizing son and rumba clave as distinct frameworks. When a cue establishes one, fills and edits preserve its relationship to the arrangement.
- Following a sectional chart, pickups, stops, repeated refrains, contrasting passages and the final return.
- Hearing bass anticipations and melodic stresses, then choosing accents that complement them.
- Following harmonic rhythm and cadences sufficiently to prepare an arrival or leave a resolving melody exposed.

A calypso passage, a lo-fi beat, a Castle waltz and an Afro-Cuban arrangement require different decisions. The chair does not impose a generic salsa pattern on every context.

For harmony, the contribution is usually structural: identifying where tension grows, where a chord change needs space and where a cadence lands. A ringing drum or bell also has audible pitch content. Select or tune it where supported, but do not turn an unpitched percussion map into a chromatic instrument by assumption.

Joe McCarthy’s own teaching materials distinguish duple/triple coordination, cáscara, bell patterns and relationships with the rhythm section. They are useful study resources for this proposed knowledge, rather than evidence that the fictional performer has already demonstrated it. [Joe McCarthy — Stylistic Coordination](https://joemccarthymusic.com/stylistic-coordination/)

## Instrument, range and articulation design
These are proposed performance requirements. Each must be matched to an available, verified library articulation before construction.

| Instrument or surface | Proposed vocabulary and range | Musical purpose | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Timbale shells | Light, even stick contacts; controlled accents; independent supporting hand | Dry time, a recurring rhythmic identity, preparation for a fuller section | Shell time needs a shell articulation. Quiet head samples are not automatically equivalent. |
| Timbale heads | Contrasting higher/lower drums, open and controlled strokes, alternating singles and bounded rolls | A short foreground phrase, answering gesture or turnaround | Treat the pair as contrasting drum registers, not an unrestricted keyboard range. |
| Timbale rims | Supported rim clicks or rimshots with deliberate accent hierarchy | Clear punctuation and ensemble cues | A rimshot and a small rim click need different sounds and dynamics. |
| Mounted bell | Contrasting body/mouth or damped/open colors where available; stable repeated figures | Time during a chosen section, lift or a recognizable rhythmic hook | Confirm the sampled bell and its actual striking zones. |
| Bongos | Higher macho and lower hembra registers; open tones, muted contacts, finger and hand articulation; martillo-informed vocabulary | Light interlocking movement, a melodic rhythm answer or a small featured passage | Bongo technique is separately learned. Conga slaps and stick-written timbale figures do not transfer unchanged. |
| Güiro | Deliberate long and short scrapes, differing intensity and clear rests | A soft moving texture or a recognizable scrape figure | A scrape has internal duration; MIDI note length may not control the recorded scrape. |
| Handbell | Controlled open/damped strokes and purposeful variation in striking area | A selected bell passage or response | Holding, striking and damping occupy physical resources. |
| Claves, woodblock, shaker or maracas | Clear attacks or shaped shake gestures appropriate to the selected object | Sparse pulse, a short answer or a contrasting section color | Each object needs its own grip, motion, decay and reference. |

## Time, dynamics and phrase construction
Begin each future part by naming its function: groove, hook, answer, transition, texture or rest. Then define its relationship to the lead and bass.

The player should be able to preserve a stable underlying feel while varying surface, accent weight, density and phrase ending. Planned timing relationships belong to the whole rhythm section. Independent random swing or timing jitter on congas, bell, bongos and producer drums can destroy their interlock.

A useful phrase has an intention and an endpoint. Before writing a fill, specify:

- The musical event it prepares.
- Where the fill begins and lands.
- Which player keeps the time during it.
- Which other parts withdraw.
- What silence or simpler texture follows.

A fill is not automatically needed every four or eight bars. A short rest can make the return more readable than another flourish.

For dynamics, begin with soft supporting time, a slightly more present answer and a bounded featured passage. Write phrase-level rise and release as well as individual accents. MIDI velocity is only a control input: its relationship to loudness, attack and sample layers must be auditioned.

The desired tone retains skin, shell, wood or metal identity without a tiring transient edge. Warm lo-fi production can come from placement, compact sounds and space; hiss, distortion and pitch instability are optional treatments requiring a purpose.

## Composition and improvisation
This guest can bring a rhythmic motif to the writing session, propose an instrumental exchange, suggest removing a redundant part or identify a stronger sectional entrance.

A percussion-led hook should be recognizable through its onset pattern, surface sequence, accents and rests. It need not compete with the approved melody. Where a melodic hook already exists, the guest studies its breathing points and helps make its return clear.

Improvisation is bounded by the written song:

- Keep the selected hook and landing points stable.
- Allow limited alternatives in explicitly designated answer or fill spaces.
- Preserve the chosen take and its exact edits.
- Write the selected result into the arrangement rather than leaving uncontrolled variation across renders.

A full song needs written sections and purposeful contrast. Moving the same percussion loop between tracks is insufficient development. No new notes or patterns are commissioned by this profile.

## Collaboration and when this guest is needed
| Existing collaborator | Shared decision |
|---|---|
| Afro-Caribbean percussionist | Decide who plays congas and who supplies the complementary bell, shell or bongo voice. Exchange phrase ownership; avoid duplicated fills. |
| Traditional/world percussionist | Identify the actual tradition and instrument in use. A tabla, udu or djembe passage may already provide all the rhythmic conversation needed. |
| Acoustic drummer | Decide whether kit time, shell time or hand percussion leads. Reduce competing snare/rim and upper-percussion activity. |
| Bass player | Agree on pulse, anticipation, note length and which accents should connect. Leave the bass figure audible. |
| Producer | Choose the groove leader and timing relationship. Retain editable acoustic/percussion sources before any chopping or processing. |
| Principal melodic player | Place answers after meaningful phrases and withdraw before exposed hook returns or speech-sensitive passages. |

Call the guest when an essential simultaneous part exceeds the existing player’s physical capacity, or when a distinct specialist voice improves the song. The existing Afro-Caribbean member can change instruments between sections when time permits.

Do not add the guest merely because timbales, bongos and güiro appear in the library. The traditional member, drummer and producer may all rest while a conga–bass–timbales exchange leads; another section may need none of those percussion parts.

## Physical performance and overdubs
Every section names the active instrument, player and performance configuration.

A timbalero can use one stick on a mounted bell while the other plays a complementary drum part, subject to the written movements. A two-handed roll consumes both hands during that passage. Independent additional bell activity then requires a changed part, another player or a declared overdub.

A hand-played bongo figure requires its own hand allocation. A held güiro normally uses one hand to support it and another to scrape it. Simultaneous two-handed bongos and held güiro require separate performance layers.

Instrument changes need a written gap for putting down sticks, picking up an auxiliary or moving position. Assess the gap at the actual tempo and layout; there is no universal switch duration.

Studio layering is available. Label each overdub explicitly, preserve its separate source and distinguish it from a plausible single-player performance. Multiple tracks may represent one playable setup, while one MIDI track may contain an impossible number of simultaneous actions.

## Tone, EQ and listening
The existing four auditions share recorded native EQ settings: high-pass at 120 Hz, a −1 dB bell at 3.2 kHz with Q 1.4, and a −1 dB shelf at 8.5 kHz. Retain these as the tested settings, not a universal prescription.

Future balancing should first address touch, density, register and level. Preserve useful drum body and bell identity. Shorten or damp a sound only when the instrument and available articulation support the intended result.

Under the existing owner permission, the four new project auditions were sent to the connected Gemini listening service. Each submission was approximately 12.143 seconds. No reference recording or family voice was uploaded.

The service generally described crisp attacks and short decays, and raised possible transient-edge concerns. These are advisory hypotheses. Its instrument labels do not verify articulations, and an isolated clip cannot establish speech intelligibility, full-song fatigue or ensemble balance.

One reported cowbell “accent strike” at 3.21 seconds is unsupported by the MIDI event list. A numerical check of the surrounding ±100 ms window found a peak near −70.4 dBFS. Do not convert that comment into a proven defect or an automatic compression decision. No human listening acceptance is claimed.

## Failure modes and acceptance questions
| Failure mode | Required review question |
|---|---|
| Every percussionist plays continuously | Can the arrangement explain each active part’s contribution, and identify complete rests? |
| Guest duplicates congas or producer accents | Does removing the guest lose an intentional musical function? |
| Impressive fill obscures the hook | Is the hook’s return clearer after the fill, with a readable landing and release? |
| Wrong articulation is hidden by EQ or velocity | Does the source actually provide the required surface and stroke? |
| Unrelated swing settings pull the rhythm apart | Do bass, congas, bell and producer parts share an intentional timing relationship? |
| Impossible simultaneous actions | Can the written hand allocation work, or is the overdub explicitly declared? |
| Instrument switch has no recovery time | Is the change feasible at the selected tempo and setup? |
| Brightness supplies all the perceived energy | Does the part retain rhythmic identity at modest volume and on the phone speaker? |
| Identical short pattern repeats through the song | Do surface, density, role, phrase ending or silence change with a musical purpose? |
| AI opinion becomes an acceptance claim | Is the observation corroborated, and are human, ensemble and device checks still recorded separately? |

Before accepting a future candidate, answer:

1. Can the rhythmic contribution be recognized without overpowering the approved melody?
2. Are shell, head, rim, bell, hand-drum and scrape identities credible?
3. Are dynamics shaped across phrases as well as individual hits?
4. Do congas, bass and guest percussion interlock without redundant activity?
5. Are all switches, simultaneous actions and overdubs accounted for?
6. Does the full mix preserve speech, touch feedback and reward meanings?
7. Does the cue remain comfortable and legible through two wraps, mono playback and the required device session?
8. Has the owner reviewed the actual arrangement rather than only its profile, preset name or measurements?
