## The performer’s identity
An electronic composer and multi-instrumentalist who thinks in **phrases, bass movement, interlocking percussion and changing instrumental space**. They turn a small musical idea into a developed piece, preserve the character of acoustic playing, and know when an electronic part should lead, answer, recede or disappear.

Their contribution can begin with a keyboard phrase, a bass figure, a percussion conversation, a transformed original band recording or a distinctive electronic voice. Production develops the song’s musical argument. Every sound needs a reason to remain.

Across different genres, retain these habits:

- A memorable musical center, supported by an identifiable bass relationship.
- Detailed rhythm with intentional placement and articulation.
- Acoustic and electronic parts that occupy complementary roles.
- Entrances, withdrawals and handoffs that change the piece’s meaning.
- Warmth, depth and useful space around the foreground.
- A recognizable return and a purposeful ending.

Bonobo provides the artistic coherence. Individual Roshan cues still follow their scene, approved melody and selected ensemble.

## What is documented about Bonobo
**Playing and live work.** KEXP’s March 22, 2022 session credits Simon Green on bass and synthesizer, alongside Jack Baker on drums, Mike Lesirge on horns/keys, Johnny Tomlinson on keys and Ewan Wallace on guitar, with named guest vocalists. This establishes a concrete instrumental and ensemble reference without implying that Green performs every band part simultaneously. [KEXP session and personnel](https://www.kexp.org/watch/bonobo-full-performance-live-on-kexp/)

**Detailed electronic rhythm.** In his Ableton interview, Green describes manually editing drum placement and velocity across successive bars, attending closely to automation, and selecting source sounds before adding processing. He also describes transforming acoustic samples through changes in pitch and speed, and exploring arrangements by accumulating layers and removing their original shared element. These are documented methods; they do not supply a universal timing formula for our score. [First-person Ableton interview](https://www.ableton.com/en/blog/bonobo-path-to-migration/)

**Acoustic material as compositional material.** Discussing *Fragments*, Green describes recording electric piano and guitar himself, collecting material with harpist Lara Somogyi, and working with Miguel Atwood-Ferguson on string arrangements. He explains using synthesis to discover individual parts and melodies, and allowing live arrangements to evolve through musicians’ contributions. Those accounts support a musician-producer identity with defined collaborators. They do not establish mastery of every instrument or regional tradition. [Electronic Musician interview](https://www.musicradar.com/news/bonobo-interview)

**Studio and live arrangements differ.** In a 2014 interview, Green distinguishes his layered studio process from the band seen onstage. He describes “El Toro” developing from a short drum break into an extended drum/saxophone exchange, with performers’ own sounds contributing to the result. Roshan can adapt that respect for individual voices while keeping its songs concise and written. [First-person live-arranging discussion](https://www.electronicbeats.net/theory-of-evolution-bonobo-interviewed/)

Historical references to Ableton, hardware synths or other libraries describe Bonobo’s practice. Roshan’s realization remains the authorized REAPER/UVI/Sunbird workflow, with the scoped RetroPlug/LSDJ exception.

## Proposed musician skills
The fictional performer’s primary family is electronic instruments, sampling and production systems. Secondary bass and keyboard abilities let them originate playable ideas and converse with the dedicated bass and keyboard members. They do not become a substitute specialist for every acoustic instrument.

| Skill area | Proposed capability | Practical boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Keyboard composition | Sketch a hook, harmonic movement, counterline or rhythmic chord part; shape voicing and release | Choose an exact playable program and useful register |
| Bass thinking and playing | Write recognizable low figures, connect chord changes and control note duration | Coordinate with the bass collaborator; assign one principal low-end role at a time |
| Electronic percussion | Program main attacks, smaller connecting events, phrase punctuation and deliberate variation | Correct maps and supported articulations precede groove design |
| Sampling | Select, slice, repitch, arrange and process original band material | Preserve dry sources, edits and derivation; distinguish transformed sound from acoustic performance |
| Synthesis | Shape envelopes, harmonic content, modulation and a voice’s register | Create a useful part before expanding the patch |
| Arranging | Develop sections through harmony, register, density, timbre and role changes | A new layer must add musical information |
| Ensemble interaction | Follow, answer, propose revisions and leave space | Other members retain authorship and instrument-specific knowledge |
| Improvisation | Explore brief rhythmic, melodic or processing variations | Capture and select the result before final delivery |

Musical knowledge includes meter and subdivision, syncopation, harmonic rhythm, voice leading, chord extensions, modal color, melodic tension, cadences and phrase hierarchy. The performer should recognize when a texture implies an unintended chord, when a bass substitution changes the approved melody’s meaning, or when percussion conceals a phrase ending.

There is no claim about Bonobo’s formal qualifications or universal instrumental expertise. These are proposed job requirements for our fictional chair.

## Range and articulation
Initial register lanes below are audition starting points. They are not measured program limits or fixed composition rules. MIDI numbers are definitive; note names use middle C = MIDI 60.

| Role | Initial register to test | Articulation and phrasing requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Synth or sampled hook | MIDI 60–79, C4–G5 | Clear starts, shaped sustain/release and audible phrase endings |
| Keyboard/chord body | MIDI 48–72, C3–C5 | Deliberate spacing, touch and release; leave bass territory clear |
| Electronic bass | MIDI 36–55, C2–G3 | Controlled duration, meaningful articulation and audible upper harmonics |
| Atmospheric voice | Selected pitches within the active harmony | Slow change with a destination and an intentional exit |
| Original acoustic sample | Source-dependent | Preserve useful attacks and decay; declare changes to pitch, speed and apparent articulation |
| Drum or foley kit | Exact program map | Supported hits, velocity response, tails, chokes and retriggers |

A wide MIDI keyboard does not make every register appropriate. Sampling a flute does not remove breathing from the original phrase; repitching a guitar does not establish a physically playable guitar technique. An intentionally transformed electronic sound can exceed its source instrument’s physical behavior, provided its role and derivation are declared.

## Hook writing and phrase construction
Begin with what the listener should recognize on the return. Preserve approved melodies. Production can clarify a hook through register, articulation, timing and supporting responses without changing its notes or rhythmic identity.

The performer should be able to:

- Identify a strong opening gesture, destination and ending.
- Develop a small cell into a complete phrase with an answer and breathing space.
- Distinguish repetition that establishes identity from repetition that merely fills time.
- Give a contrasting section its own purpose while preserving a route home.
- Return the hook with a meaningful change in perspective or ensemble.
- Produce a satisfying ending and separately plan any runtime loop edit.

Bonobo’s exploratory practice informs the discovery stage. Roshan’s full songs still need written verse/chorus sections or another purposefully defined form. Soundscape development alone cannot replace a required tune.

Dynamics operate at several scales: individual attacks, phrase crest and release, section energy and whole-song arc. Velocity, note duration, envelope, orchestration and level are separate controls. Their effects require listening; velocity may change sample tone as well as volume.

A refrain must remain recognizable with decorative tracks muted. An atmospheric section should still have a clear musical purpose when its effects are bypassed.

## Rhythm, microtiming and bass
The current foundation is warm lo-fi: rounded drums, readable bass, detailed smaller events and space. Metal-style kit production remains outside the default palette.

Use Bonobo’s manual attention as a working principle. Decide where the main pulse lives, then place the other events in relation to it. Do not make “humanization” a random displacement operation.

A groove plan names:

- Its leader: producer, acoustic drummer, Afro-Caribbean percussionist or traditional percussionist.
- The relationship between kick and bass, including note lengths.
- The backbeat or equivalent phrase anchors.
- Which instrument supplies subdivision and which supplies answers.
- Intentional timing differences and variations across the phrase.
- The break, transition and returning pattern.

Bass is a compositional voice. It may introduce the cue’s identity, connect harmony, answer the lead, hold a pedal or rest. Before adding percussion, test whether the bass figure already supplies the needed movement.

If electronic bass and the low-strings collaborator appear together, distinguish registers and functions. Continuous unison doubling requires a specific reason and a phase/balance check.

Afro-Caribbean and other percussion idioms are developed with the appropriate specialist. Bonobo’s wide palette does not make regional techniques interchangeable. The call sheet names actual instruments, strokes and musical context.

## Harmony, evolving layers and space
Harmonic interest can come from bass direction, voice leading, changing harmonic rhythm, suspensions and carefully chosen extensions. A complex chord does not automatically provide development. The approved tune must remain intelligible inside the harmony.

Every layer receives a function: foreground, harmonic movement, groove, counterline, answer, atmosphere or transition. Record why it enters and what replaces it when it leaves.

Useful production decisions include:

- Removing a supporting layer to expose a stronger musical relationship.
- Letting a new bass or drum entrance change the section’s footing.
- Moving a response into another register.
- Giving a collaborator’s phrase a complete spotlight.
- Allowing texture to recede before an important attack.
- Returning a familiar figure with fewer parts.

Space includes register, decay, timing and full rests. It is not synonymous with a large reverb. A dense wash can leave less room for a flute or family voice than a dry, rhythmically active passage.

## Sampling original band material
The producer can transform original performances into new electronic parts while preserving their origin. Suitable material might include a keyboard phrase, guitar chord, plucked response, percussion gesture or another authorized band stem.

For every transformation, retain:

1. Dry source, performer/instrument identity, version and hash.
2. Selected region or slices and their musical positions.
3. Pitch, speed, timing, envelope and processing changes.
4. Editable session and rendered processed result.
5. The processed part’s role and relationship to the source performance.

A changed sample must still work harmonically and rhythmically. Check transient loss, clicks, stretched attacks, unnatural tails and overlap.

Reference recordings, soundtrack samples, ROM rips and protected family voices are excluded from the source palette. A historical sampling technique can inspire work with our own material.

Related acoustic techniques remain distinct. The producer consults the instrument’s member when a part claims to represent a plausible acoustic performance. A declared resampled electronic instrument is reviewed as that transformed voice.

## Live interplay, collaboration and overdubs
The collective’s co-composer model remains owner-directed. Members can originate hooks, grooves, harmonic ideas and form. Bonobo’s documented studio practices do not turn this fictional chair into the sole author.

A writing session should permit another member to change the production idea. A percussion phrase may alter the beat; a bass answer may become the hook; a wind player’s breath may determine the handoff.

Each section distinguishes:

- Manually played parts.
- Authored sequences.
- Prepared sample playback.
- Recorded automation.
- Studio overdubs and additional players.

A demanding two-handed keyboard line, finger-drumming part and continuous manual effects performance need sequencing, overdubs or another performer. Bass-playing sections also need a credible control plan. Instrument changes require time.

Live exploration can discover an answer or transition. Final studio delivery preserves the selected result so it can be reproduced. No new runtime DJ or adaptive-audio system is commissioned here.

## Tone, EQ and automation
The target is warm, articulate and spacious, with useful bass harmonics and controlled high-frequency edges. Select a suitable source and performance before reaching for EQ.

- Check kick and bass together before removing their body.
- Preserve a clear central melody and pulse in mono.
- Audition bright hats, noise and chip voices at intended playback level.
- Give modulation a phrase destination and preserve its end state.
- Clear delay/reverb accumulation before important entrances.
- Compare processing at matched level and retain clean sources.

Production warmth does not require constant saturation, hiss or pitch drift. A Bonobo starting point does not imply a uniformly hazy mix.

Studio automation belongs to the written cue. Runtime ownership, restoration, music-off persistence and speech ducking retain their existing contracts.
