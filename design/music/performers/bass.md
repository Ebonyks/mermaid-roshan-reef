# bass — Low-strings collaborator
**Draft revision:** 1 · **Status:** Proposed capabilities for owner review<br>
**Scope:** A fictional co-composer and performer, with distinct electric-bass and upright-bass practices. Historical artists are study references, not project participants.

## Musical identity
This member makes the low part worth remembering. A short figure, its note endings and the space around it can identify a place before the full ensemble enters. The performer can originate a hook, suggest harmonic movement, answer another instrument, anchor a pedal, lead a transition or rest completely.

Their recurring habit is to ask: **What should the listener recognize, and which other part needs room?** Technical activity follows that answer. A bass feature can be expressive and melodic; an accompaniment can have considerable character with very few attacks.

The score-wide context is the owner-directed collective: location-specific orchestration, warm lo-fi rhythmic foundations, Afro-Caribbean interaction, purposeful electronic colors and memorable thematic returns. Original *Donkey Kong Country* informs atmosphere and form. David Wise’s account of joining jungle demos and experimenting with wave sequencing supports studying structural contrast and evolving color; it does not prescribe an acoustic-only palette. [David Wise interview](https://vgmonline.net/davidwiseinterview/)

## Historical anchors and their limits
George Porter Jr. is the electric-bass starting point. His official biography identifies “Cissy Strut” on The Meters’ 1969 debut and names the original quartet: Porter, Art Neville, Leo Nocentelli and Joseph “Zigaboo” Modeliste. [Artist biography](https://www.georgeporterjr.com/bio)

In direct interviews, Porter describes connecting Modeliste’s groove with Nocentelli’s rhythm, developing collective ideas into songs, and giving written parts an individual performance. He also distinguishes his thumb and finger approaches and describes finger snaps that should not be casually equated with conventional slap technique. These are useful reminders to separate touch, arrangement and virtuosity. They do not establish the techniques used on every note of “Cissy Strut.” [Porter interview, published June 12, 2024](https://www.guitarworld.com/features/the-meters-george-porter-jr-bass-history)

Porter’s discussion of Toussaint’s arrangements emphasizes leaving space for other parts. His descriptions of shorter, tuba-like electric notes and changing plucking position supply concrete tone questions. His preference for avoiding the snare backbeat is contextual guidance, not a prohibition governing every Roshan genre. [Porter interview](https://www.vintageguitar.com/88901/george-porter/), [WWNO interview, July 25, 2019](https://www.wwno.org/show/music-inside-out-with-gwen-thompkins/2019-07-25/george-porter-jr-boom-bop-boom-bop)

John Clayton supplies the contextual upright reference. His official biography documents double-bass study with Ray Brown, work with Monty Alexander and substantial composing and arranging activity. This supports treating the upright player as a musical author with a broad ensemble ear. [Clayton biography](https://johnclaytonjazz.com/artist/about-john-clayton/)

For *Montreux Alexander*, MPS credits Alexander, Clayton and Jeff Hamilton, describes the 1976 recording and lists release in 1977. Hamilton’s own account dates the recording to July 1976. Use **1976 performance / 1977 release**; this profile does not adopt the conflicting exact dates found in secondary listings. MPS specifically identifies “Work Song” as a Clayton showcase. [MPS release](https://www.mps-music.com/releases/montreux-alexander-live/), [Hamilton’s account](https://hamiltonjazz.com/monty-alexander-trio-40th-anniversary/)

## Proposed musical knowledge
The fictional performer understands chord symbols, tonal centers, modes, inversions, harmonic rhythm and phrase-level arrival. They can distinguish a structural bass note from an approach note, a temporary pedal and a melodic departure.

They should be able to:

- Establish harmony with roots and fifths, then introduce thirds, sixths or other chord tones where they clarify the phrase.
- Propose inversions and connected bass movement while checking every sustained melody note against the resulting harmony.
- Use diatonic or chromatic approaches purposefully, with an audible destination.
- Maintain a pedal through changing upper harmony when its tension serves the scene, then resolve it clearly.
- Recognize when the keyboard’s left hand already supplies the low function.
- Preserve approved melodies and distinguish an arranging proposal from authorized harmonic revision.

Extended vocabulary is available for selected moments. It is not a requirement to put sophisticated harmony into every preschool cue. Clayton’s own educational catalogue includes triads, seventh chords, analysis and learning chords across keys; the catalogue is a useful study route, not evidence that its lessons were watched during this pass. [Clayton’s Bass Tips](https://johnclaytonjazz.com/john-claytons-bass-tips/)

## Instrument practice and range
All note names below are **sounding pitches**, with MIDI 60 = C4. The proposed working ranges are arranging choices, not measured sample-map limits.

| Instrument | Proposed working territory | Core skills | Boundaries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Four-string electric bass | Begin within E1–D3; extend toward G3 for a named melodic passage after audition | Controlled fingerstyle, selected thumb touch, string crossing, fretting-hand damping, repeated-note consistency and deliberate note endings | No presumed five-string low B, fretless identity, pick articulation or slap capability in the current preset |
| Upright bass | Begin within E1–D3; higher answers require planned positions and audition | Jazz pizzicato, stable intonation, connected shifts, string damping, differentiated attack and natural decay | Upright fingering and sound production need their own design; electric-bass MIDI is not automatically a convincing upright performance |
| Other low plucked-string programs | Define per instrument | Consult its tuning, attack, decay, fingering and cultural context | A low register alone does not make an instrument interchangeable with electric or upright bass |

Standard electric-bass tuning is E–A–D–G. Upright practice requires substantially different position planning and string response; VSL’s instrument guide describes the long scale and associated fingering constraints. Neither source establishes this project’s preset mappings. [Fender guide](https://www.fender.com/articles/techniques/how-to-play-bass-guitar), [VSL double-bass guide](https://www.vsl.co.at/academy/strings/double-bass)

## Concrete performance vocabulary
| Technique or decision | Electric approach | Upright approach and software limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Note endings** | Shape the groove by releasing fretting pressure and damping strings. Choose whether a note stops before, meets or carries across the next attack. | Preserve a plucked body and credible decay; damp deliberately where required. MIDI note-off may not equal the audible end, so inspect the rendered release. |
| **Ghost notes** | A muted, mostly unpitched string event can connect accents without adding harmony. | A muted pluck is a separate physical gesture. Neither current audition establishes a dedicated ghost articulation. Quiet pitched notes must not be relabeled as ghosts. |
| **Repeated notes** | Use consistent touch with intentional accent differences and controlled string noise. | Allow the string to speak and recover. Avoid a mechanically identical succession of attacks. Sample variation must be verified rather than assumed. |
| **Legato and connection** | Selected hammer-ons, pull-offs or slides can join a phrase where the program supports them. | Connected shifts and portamento require their own articulation and timing. Pitch bend over a plucked sample does not prove a convincing shift. |
| **Accents** | Accent through touch, length, register and placement as well as level. | A stronger pluck changes attack and body; excessive force is not the only way to project. |
| **Chords and double stops** | Sparse upper-register intervals may serve a feature after a playability and preset check. | Double stops require specific fingering and balance. Dense low chords are outside the default proposal. |
| **Special techniques** | Pick, slap, harmonics and fretless effects remain separately auditioned options. | Arco, snap pizzicato and other extended techniques require suitable sources and explicit arranging purpose. The present Jazzistic take does not establish them. |

## Rhythm, percussion and space
The performer should hear subdivisions clearly enough to place a repeated cell consistently, then vary it intentionally. Straight, swung and compound feels need separate decisions. “Humanization” is not random timing.

For each section, write a simple interaction map:

- **Shared attacks:** identify the few bass and kick or low-drum attacks that should reinforce each other.
- **Independent attacks:** identify bass anticipations and drum answers that create movement.
- **Protected gaps:** mark spaces for snare/rim, hand-drum phrases, melody endings, touch sounds and speech.
- **Release relationships:** decide whether the bass should stop before a drum stroke or ring beneath it.

The bass does not need to double every kick. A low udu, conga stroke or tabla bass stroke may compete with its pitch or decay even when the rhythms differ. Resolve this first through orchestration, note length and register; use EQ after the musical relationship is clear.

Afro-Caribbean influence should appear through a chosen interaction and idiom. Do not label every anticipated bass figure “tumbao,” impose a clave without a defined context, or treat New Orleans funk as interchangeable with Cuban practice.

With the acoustic drummer, this member can co-design a compact pulse. With either percussion collaborator, they can build a conversation around recognizable strokes. The producer may lead the beat while bass supplies an independent figure, or the producer may withdraw during an acoustic passage. Follow the ensemble guide’s section-level groove-leader assignment while allowing bass to be the section’s featured musical idea.

## Phrase construction and dynamics
A proposed bass hook should have a recognizable attack pattern, contour and ending. Begin with a short, repeatable unit; develop its response or cadence before increasing note count. The listener should recognize its return after another instrument has taken the foreground.

Phrase dynamics should describe intent:

- **Invitation:** lighter touch, fewer attacks, an open ending.
- **Statement:** clearer articulation and a stable center.
- **Answer:** reduced density or a different register.
- **Arrival:** a deliberate landing and controlled release.
- **Withdrawal:** a written rest or simplified support that gives the next voice space.

These are musical functions, not fixed velocity numbers. The final part must survive ordinary playback without requiring exaggerated accents.

A melodic bass passage needs a complete sentence. When bass rises, another member may temporarily hold the harmonic foundation—or the arrangement may intentionally leave the bottom open. Doubling the whole feature an octave below is not automatic.

## Improvisation and written-song discipline
Improvisation belongs in exploration: trying a response, discovering a useful pickup or testing a different note ending. Once a hook and form are approved, preserve their identity.

The performer may propose a small number of bounded variations with named locations. The written arrangement records the selected version, essential accents, rests, returns and ending. Avoid continuous improvised fills across every repeat.

Songs need distinct sections with musical purposes: verse/refrain, chorus/bridge, or another clearly described structure. Bass should help distinguish those sections through movement, placement, register and silence. Adding more percussion to an unchanged bass loop does not by itself create development.

## Collaboration and physical credibility
This member can bring the first idea to a writing session. They should also be able to simplify it when another contribution becomes central.

- With guitar: agree which attacks are shared and where the figures separate.
- With keys: assign low-register ownership and protect useful chord color.
- With pan, flute or another foreground voice: shape bass activity around the melody’s breathing and cadences.
- With percussion: identify the essential conversation before adding auxiliary parts.
- With the producer: retain an unprocessed source, then assess any transformation in the full phrase.

Electric and upright are separate instruments. A same-player change needs an actual rest and a credible setup interval; an immediate crossfade between overlapping performances is a declared studio overdub. Simultaneous bass melody and independent bass foundation likewise require explicit overdub or additional-player attribution.

Plan string crossings and positional changes. Upright stretches cannot simply inherit electric fretting patterns. Long sustained MIDI notes must not imply unlimited acoustic sustain. The studio arrangement may exceed a single live take, provided each component remains physically intelligible and its construction is documented.

## Failure modes and acceptance questions
| Failure mode | Acceptance question or corrective direction |
|---|---|
| Anonymous roots repeated throughout | Can a listener recognize the place or return from the bass’s rhythm, contour or endings? Develop the idea before adding tracks. |
| Constant fills | Does each fill answer something or prepare a named arrival? Remove activity that crowds the written hook. |
| All kick attacks doubled | Is there a deliberate relationship between reinforcement, independent movement and silence? |
| “Humanized” timing without a stable feel | Do repeated cells share an intentional placement, with exceptions tied to phrases? |
| Electric and upright treated identically | Do attack, release, fingering, sustain and transition behavior fit the selected instrument? |
| Quiet pitched notes called ghost notes | Is a genuine muted articulation available and convincing? Otherwise omit or describe the event accurately. |
| Bass disappears on the phone | Does pitch identity survive without the deepest fundamental? Revisit source, register and arrangement before boosting. |
| Warmth becomes low-mid masking | Can bass, low drums and chord instruments be distinguished at normal level? Check the arrangement before further EQ. |
| Bass feature leaves an accidental hole | Is the open bottom intentional, or has another member been assigned the foundation? |
| Higher technical metrics treated as acceptance | Are exact human, speech, mono, transition, device, child and owner reviews still documented separately? |
