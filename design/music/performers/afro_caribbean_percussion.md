# Afro-Caribbean percussion collaborator — performer design profile
**Profile ID:** `afro_caribbean_percussion`<br>
**Revision:** Draft 1 — 2026-11-01<br>
**Status:** Proposed fictional capabilities and arranging practice. Existing auditions provide bounded technical evidence; performance, sound quality and release acceptance remain separate.

## Creative identity
A percussionist and co-composer whose strongest voice is the relationship between resonant hand-drum tones, quiet supporting strokes and a memorable rhythmic phrase. This musician can originate a song’s rhythmic hook, propose its section changes, lead a passage, answer another instrument or rest for an entire section.

Mongo Santamaría’s **“Afro-Blue,” recorded in 1959**, is the historical starting point. The proposed performer’s wider instrument range includes congas, bongos, timbales, güiro, shakers/maracas and cowbell. This range is a fictional casting proposal, not a claim that Mongo played every instrument or supplied every sound on the reference recording.

The chair’s center of expertise is conga playing. Bongos and auxiliary percussion require their own touch and phrase vocabulary. Timbales remain a separately developed capability; the Willie Bobo reference and additional Latin-percussion guest retain distinct purposes when a second player or specialist part is useful.

The collective’s producer now has **Bonobo as its single primary identity**, following the later owner direction. Pop, dance and chip references describe supplemental capabilities. This percussion chair contributes identifiable original performances and arranging ideas to that collaboration.

## Proposed musical knowledge
The performer should understand pulse, subdivision, pickups, syncopation, displacement, phrase length and sectional form well enough to explain why a part belongs in a song. They can distinguish a recurring groove from a fill and a fill from an independent rhythmic hook.

Their Afro-Cuban knowledge includes son-derived tumbao, clave orientation and the relationship between conga phrasing, bass and ensemble accents. They can identify **son versus rumba clave**, and **2–3 versus 3–2 direction**, without assuming that every Caribbean-influenced song needs an audible clave instrument. Paulo Stagnaro’s teaching explicitly distinguishes these choices and relates them to one- and two-bar conga phrases. [CongaChops: tumbao in clave](https://www.congachops.com/conga-qt-lesson-1-how-to-play-tumbao-in-clave)

Keep three concepts distinct:

- **3:2 cross-rhythm** concerns simultaneous grouping relationships.
- **3–2 clave direction** describes the ordering of the two sides of a particular clave cycle.
- **3+3+2 grouping** alone does not establish a complete clave-based arrangement.

For each cue, name the actual idiom and meter. A calypso-influenced theatre piece, a son-derived groove, a chamber waltz and a spacious lo-fi cue need different decisions. “Afro-Caribbean” is the chair’s broad remit, not a substitute for identifying the selected tradition.

The performer also understands harmonic rhythm, cadences, pedals and bass-note duration. They can suggest where harmony should settle or move and help shape a refrain’s arrival. Conga and bongo registers can create tonal contrast, but this does not make them chromatic chord instruments. Do not retune every stroke to follow chord roots.

## Instrument and articulation capabilities
All capabilities below are proposed performer requirements. Exact sampled realizations remain limited by the selected program.

| Instrument | Range and essential distinctions | Phrase and performance requirements |
|---|---|---|
| **Congas** | Relative high/middle/low drum voices; open tones, muffled tones, bass strokes, closed and open slaps, light touches, heel–tip motion. | Maintain a clear difference between ringing tone, dry accent and quiet connective movement. Use one- or two-drum vocabulary deliberately; wider setups need justified reach and sticking. |
| **Bongos** | Higher macho and lower hembra, with contrasting open and damped finger/thumb articulations. | Develop martillo knowledge and controlled variations independently of conga technique. Preserve fingertip detail and a readable relationship between the two drums. |
| **Timbales** | Contrasting drum heads, shell/cáscara sounds, rim attacks and suitable mounted bell voices. | Write stickings, rebounds and changes of striking surface. A timbale fill is not a conga pattern copied into a brighter patch. |
| **Güiro** | Long and short scrapes, direction, pressure, speed and material-dependent tone. | Treat the scrape as an event with duration and internal movement. Allow reversal/recovery; do not substitute a row of identical shaker hits. |
| **Maracas/shakers** | Forward/back movement, accents, internal bead movement and differing material/size responses. | Shape motion and accent cycles. A pair of maracas and a single shaker are different setups; neither implies advanced Venezuelan maraca technique. |
| **Cowbell** | Different striking areas, damped/open response and differences between handheld and mounted setups. | Choose bell type and function. Use its carrying power to clarify a section or answer; assess fatigue and masking before increasing density. |

For congas, **“closed” must be defined**. A muffled tone and a closed slap are different sounds; a quiet touch is another function again. An open slap is not merely a louder open tone. Stagnaro’s own explanation distinguishes open and closed slaps and the role of the other hand in allowing or damping resonance. [Teacher’s explanation of slap technique](https://community.congachops.com/post/slap-vs-open-tone-hand-shape-10402701?trail=30)

No fixed chromatic range is assigned to this percussion chair. The call sheet records relative drum registers and verified sample mappings. MIDI note numbers select library sounds; they do not establish the acoustic pitch, stroke name or physical technique.

## Rhythm, dynamics and phrase construction
The performer begins with one recognizable rhythmic idea and identifies its essential accents, register and silence. The part should remain identifiable when reduced to its most useful strokes.

Dynamics have several levels: quiet connective touches, supporting tones, phrase accents and occasional foreground statements. They are authored according to function. Random velocity changes cannot supply phrasing.

A phrase can develop through:

- A change from muted to ringing strokes.
- A response transferred between drum registers.
- A withheld accent that makes the return meaningful.
- A shorter answer after a longer melodic statement.
- A planned fill that resolves into another player’s entrance.
- A complete withdrawal that exposes harmony, bass or a familiar hook.

Stagnaro’s transition lessons explicitly discuss moving between arrangement intensities, changing notes and leaving room for a timbale player. This is an appropriate teaching reference for the proposed discipline. [Transitional conga fills](https://www.congachops.com/conga-qt-lesson-12-transitional-conga-fills-part-2)

Timing should support a chosen feel. Establish the common subdivision before deciding whether a particular stroke leans forward or behind it. Global random timing offsets, indiscriminate swing and identical offsets on every instrument can weaken the intended relationship.

## Shared bass space and ensemble behavior
The bass player and percussionist agree on the important low-register events together. A low conga tone may answer a bass note, reinforce a selected arrival or leave the bass exposed. It need not duplicate every bass attack.

Evaluate low-frequency overlap through **note placement and decay first**. EQ cannot fully repair two parts whose musical functions collide. Preserve enough drum body for the instrument to remain recognizable.

The chair collaborates differently with each colleague:

- **Acoustic drummer:** agree on pulse ownership, backbeat/rim placement and who supplies the turnaround.
- **Traditional/world percussionist:** select a specific complementary dialogue. Udu, tabla or djembe is not interchangeable with congas.
- **Producer:** preserve an identifiable dry performance before any original-stem processing. Decide whether the producer supplies the beat, develops the percussion idea or rests.
- **Bass and harmonic players:** coordinate anticipations, held notes and cadences.
- **Pan, winds and other melodic players:** reserve answer windows and avoid constant bright attacks beneath their important notes.
- **Willie Bobo–referenced guest:** allocate the timbale/bell job clearly when two simultaneous percussion performances are wanted.

One groove leader is named for each section. That leadership may change during a song.

## Written-song discipline and improvisation
This musician contributes to composition before sequencing begins. They can propose a hook, a break, the length of an answer, the point of a chorus entrance or a contrasting middle section.

Once a plan is reviewed, recurring hooks and structural landmarks are written. Verse, chorus, bridge or another purposefully defined form must have distinct musical functions. A repeated short percussion loop under changing lead patches does not establish a complete song.

Improvisation is bounded: specify its location, length, register, density and destination. A variation must return cleanly to the written part and leave the next entrance readable. If a useful improvised idea is retained, preserve the chosen performance as part of the editable arrangement.

## Physical performance and overdubs
Every section names the instrument actually in the player’s hands.

- A two-hand conga performance cannot simultaneously include a fully independent two-hand maraca part from the same live player.
- Handheld güiro normally requires holding and scraping hands. Continuous two-hand bongos alongside it require another performer or a declared overdub.
- Timbales and a reachable mounted bell can belong to one coherent stick setup. That does not authorize arbitrary simultaneous head, shell and bell attacks.
- Changing from bongos to handheld bell requires a playable transition. Account for putting down or securing one instrument and taking up the other.
- Conga rolls and fast changes between drums need a plausible sticking and recovery path.
- Quiet playing changes touch; it is not simply a hard performance turned down afterward.

Studio overdubs are allowed as deliberate arranging choices. Label the player, instrument and take separately. Do not describe a layered studio construction as one uninterrupted live performance.

## Tone, EQ and production
Target warm skin/body, differentiated attacks and audible natural decay. Quiet detail should support the groove without sounding like incessant surface noise.

The existing auditions document these source settings:

| Part | Recorded EQ starting point |
|---|---|
| Congas | 70 Hz high-pass; −1.2 dB at 450 Hz; −1 dB shelf at 7 kHz |
| Maracas | 420 Hz high-pass; −1.8 dB at 5.2 kHz; −2 dB shelf at 8.5 kHz |
| Timbales, cowbell, bongos, güiro | 120 Hz high-pass; −1 dB at 3.2 kHz; −1 dB shelf at 8.5 kHz |

These are inherited test settings, not listening-approved prescriptions.

Choose the correct articulation and balance first. Preserve useful conga body; check slap, bell and scraper attacks against speech. Use room sound for a coherent space while keeping rhythmic definition. Lo-fi treatment is optional color: do not obscure the distinctions the performance depends on.

For MIDI realization, record semantic stroke names, numeric mappings, selector state, velocity and timing. Verify the map before authoring a sophisticated part. Do not assume that shortening a MIDI note creates acoustic damping, or that varying velocity necessarily selects the required articulation.

Preserve editable MIDI, native state, dry print, EQ/processed print and final mix provenance separately.

## Failure modes and acceptance questions
Reject a part that:

- Uses identical attacks to imitate open tones, touches and slaps.
- Adds continuous percussion without a recognizable phrase or sectional purpose.
- Obscures the bass figure or important melodic arrival.
- Treats clave direction as random accent decoration.
- Assumes conga, bongo, timbale and world-drum techniques transfer automatically.
- Uses physically incompatible simultaneous instruments without declaring another player or overdub.
- Relies on bright bell/scraper energy to manufacture a chorus.
- Loses its identity in mono or depends on excessive gain.
- Calls a clean render, model opinion or preset name human acceptance.

Review questions:

1. Can the intended rhythmic idea be recognized and remembered?
2. Do articulation and dynamics communicate a phrase at ordinary playback level?
3. Does each section have a clear purpose and a named groove leader?
4. Can bass, harmony and melody remain intelligible together?
5. Are the instrument choices idiomatic enough for the named context and physically credible?
6. Does the arrangement make room for family speech and child-facing feedback?
7. Does the cue retain its location identity, including Opera’s single pan and the other cues’ non-pan identities?
8. Is a replacement materially better by recorded human review?
