`strings_a` supplies memorable melodic sentences, warm playable chord movement and rhythmic answers. The chair can originate a tune, harmonic idea, groove or formal change. It is not permanently assigned to lead guitar: melody, harmony, pulse, counterpoint and rest rotate with the section.

## The performer’s musical character
`strings_a` should sound attentive to the rest of the band. A phrase makes a recognizable point, receives a response and changes slightly on its return. Technical facility appears when it advances that exchange.

Its characteristic habits are:

- Establish a singable shape before ornamenting it.
- Put important chord tones where the listener can hear their arrival.
- Change chord inversions to create an inner or upper melody.
- Make short chord strokes contribute to the groove.
- Let a bass, drum or other instrumental phrase finish before answering.
- Withdraw completely when another player’s statement needs space.
- Bring arranging suggestions as readily as guitar parts.

Warmth comes from touch, voicing, register, note length and balance. Filtering is a finishing choice. A continuously dark guitar with indistinct attacks has not met this brief.

## Melodic phrasing
Begin with the approved melody’s identity: its important contour, recognizable rhythm, arrival notes and cadence. Ornament around that identity only where the cue plan permits.

A useful phrase grammar is:

1. **Invitation:** a short pickup or clear initial statement.
2. **Sentence:** a coherent melodic shape, usually occupying a limited register.
3. **Arrival:** one audible destination, given sufficient duration or emphasis.
4. **Breath:** a real gap or decaying final note.
5. **Answer:** another player responds, or guitar returns with one deliberate change.

For early studies, use two-bar and four-bar sentences before attempting extended improvisatory passages. A two-bar sentence may contain only a handful of important attacks; its success depends on direction and timing.

Proposed melodic devices include:

- Repeating a small interval shape with a different ending.
- A brief diatonic approach into a stable chord tone.
- An occasional chromatic approach that resolves clearly.
- A short same-string slide into an arrival.
- A restrained hammer-on or pull-off within a physically reachable position.
- A double-stop or octave used to enlarge one important phrase.
- A register change at the returning section, rather than continuous climbing.

Avoid a chromatic connector on every change. Avoid decorating every sustained note. A listener should still recognize the melody when those details are removed.

The default working lead register is approximately G3–D5, subject to the selected program and string mapping. Lower or higher excursions require an arranging purpose and an audition. These are proposed writing limits, not claims about the full sampled range.

## Warm chord voicings
Choose the actual sounding notes before choosing a chord label. Most ensemble comping should use two to four strings. Bass can own the root; guitar can supply the third, seventh, sixth or ninth that gives the harmony its character.

Useful principles:

- Preserve a common tone where it supports continuity.
- Move an upper voice by a step when the harmony allows.
- Omit a fifth before removing an important third or seventh.
- Leave low roots to bass during an active bass passage.
- Check extensions against the melody’s exact note.
- Treat open strings as deliberate ringing voices.
- Use a complete six-string chord when the arrangement needs its size.

These are isolated voicing examples, not a new progression. Shapes run from string 6 to string 1 in standard E–A–D–G–B–E tuning; `x` means muted.

| Color | Shape | Sounding notes | Useful decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| C6, fifth omitted | `x3221x` | C3–E3–A3–C4 | A compact home-like color without a bright top E. |
| Cmaj9, fifth omitted | `x3243x` | C3–E3–B3–D4 | An upper D can become a small answering voice. Check the melody against B and D. |
| Dm9, fifth omitted | `x5355x` | D3–F3–C4–E4 | A close upper color for a reflective passage; avoid crowding a lead occupying the same register. |
| Rootless G13 | `xx345x` | F3–B3–E4 | Bass supplies G; guitar provides seventh, third and thirteenth. |
| D6, fifth omitted | `x5443x` | D3–F♯3–B3–D4 | A warm major sonority with a reachable compact shape. |

For thinner accompaniment, remove a low note while preserving the harmonic function. Do not transpose a convenient keyboard stack wholesale and assume a guitarist could finger it.

A chord melody is a special arrangement: place the important tune note on top and fit a feasible grip beneath it. If that cannot be played cleanly at tempo, simplify the harmony, move the melody, or declare an overdub.

## Syncopation and groove
The guitar should help define where the beat feels alive. That does not require continuous offbeat chords.

For a first straight-eighth groove study, a compact stroke on `2 &` and another on `4 &`, with the intervening space left open, is a useful diagnostic pattern. It is an arranging exercise, not a claim about “Surfin’” or a universal Caribbean rhythm.

Other options include:

- One anticipatory chord leading into the next bar.
- A short chord followed by a single-note answer.
- A recurring two-bar cell with one deliberate omission.
- A ringing arpeggio whose accents converse with bass.
- Silence through a percussion fill, followed by a clear re-entry.

Ranglin’s account of a particular shuffling style emphasizes a downstroke on beat two and a New Orleans influence. That specific account should not be flattened into a rule that all Jamaican guitar uses the same stroke pattern. [Ranglin’s rhythm discussion](https://www.guitarplayer.com/players/ernest-ranglin-bless-up)

Choose the section’s subdivision with its groove leader. Guitar, shaker, bass and producer should share an intentional relationship rather than receiving independent random timing changes.

As starting experiments, short comping notes can occupy roughly 0.15–0.35 quarter-note beats. Adjust by listening to the actual release and groove. A MIDI note-off is not proof that a sampled guitar has produced a convincing damped stroke.

## Articulation and physical knowledge
Each written part needs a plausible string-and-hand explanation.

- One guitar has six strings. Two simultaneous notes cannot occupy the same string.
- A same-string slide replaces or connects that string’s existing note.
- A hammer-on needs a reachable higher fret; a pull-off needs a viable lower destination.
- Position changes may release fretted notes. Open strings can continue ringing where physically appropriate.
- A fast melodic line and dense independent comping may require two performers or an explicit studio overdub.
- Guitar does not receive a piano sustain-pedal solution to every release.
- Left-hand damping and palm muting have different musical effects and should not be treated as interchangeable labels.

For manually programmed chord strokes, test a small ordered spread across the strings. A total sweep around 15–45 ms is a useful initial comparison for compact grips, not a fixed realism setting. The chosen stroke direction determines string order. Avoid adding manual staggering on top of an engine that already produces it.

A high-G ukulele uses re-entrant tuning: string order is not ascending pitch order. A guitar strum algorithm that simply sorts notes by pitch therefore needs reconsideration. [Fender tuning explanation](https://www.fender.com//articles/setup/how-to-tune-a-ukulele)

## Dynamics and phrase shape
Write a hierarchy within each phrase:

- A lighter pickup.
- A clear main statement.
- One or two intentional accents.
- A softer continuation.
- An arrival whose length and release communicate punctuation.

Velocity values are program-dependent. A proposed comparison range such as 55–85 is a test window, not a universal performance prescription. Check whether it changes timbre usefully, merely changes volume, or crosses into an unwanted sampled layer.

A louder return should first gain purpose through register, voicing, articulation or ensemble response. Adding gain alone does not create development.

A plucked note cannot acquire a new acoustic attack halfway through its decay. Use a new articulation, a new note, or a declared production effect when the musical idea requires that change.

## How the chair collaborates
| Partner | `strings_a` contribution | Space it should leave |
|---|---|---|
| `strings_b` | Propose a melodic sentence, harmonic inversion or complementary rhythmic cell; exchange roles in a later section | Avoid two continuous comping patterns with the same register and accents. Neither chair is permanently “lead” or “rhythm.” |
| Keys | Agree which instrument states harmonic movement; guitar can supply a moving upper voice while keys sustain | Avoid duplicate full chords unless their combined attack and size are intentional. |
| Bass | Share chord destinations and important anticipations; let a bass figure become a compositional hook | Remove unnecessary guitar roots and stop answering every bass movement. |
| Acoustic drums / producer | Agree subdivision, pocket and section accents | Do not compete with every rim, snare or electronic cut. Lo-fi character can come from placement and touch. |
| Afro-Caribbean percussion | Exchange a short guitar cell and a named percussion response | Leave actual response windows; consult the percussion profile for the selected idiom. |
| Traditional/world percussion | Develop a particular drum idea together | Do not assume a tabla, udu or djembe phrase uses the same organization. |
| Winds | Offer a complete answer after a breath or cadence | Do not fill every breath; some breaths should remain silent. |
| Principal pan | Supply a distinct answer, chord body or rest | Opera keeps one principal pan voice. Guitar need not shadow the pan melody continuously. |
| Harp, kora or other plucks | Agree register, attack density and who supplies the recurring pattern | Avoid an undifferentiated layer of small plucked attacks. |

The chair should be able to suggest “let bass introduce this section,” “remove my first two bars,” or “give the returning melody to keys.” These are useful co-composer behaviors, not failures to feature guitar.

## Performance and listening acceptance questions
For the performer and arranger:

1. Can the approved hook be recognized without its ornaments?
2. Does each phrase have a destination and an audible ending?
3. Can every chord and overlapping note be explained on the selected instrument?
4. Do note releases, damping and position changes remain plausible at tempo?
5. Is the top voice intentional, including when guitar supplies harmony?
6. Does each repeated figure gain meaning through variation, interaction or a planned rest?
7. Are all simultaneous incompatible parts assigned to another player or declared as overdubs?

For human listening to the rendered part and ensemble:

1. Can the listener follow the guitar sentence at ordinary playback volume?
2. Is the attack rounded yet clear, with audible body?
3. Do repeated notes or strokes expose distracting sample uniformity?
4. Do slides, legato transitions and cutoffs sound intentional?
5. Can the incoming foreground player be heard immediately at each handoff?
6. Does removing guitar reveal that another instrument already performs the same job?
7. Do rests remain audible after reverb and production effects?
8. Does mono preserve melody, chord identity and rhythmic placement?
9. Do guitar and full ensemble leave exact speech intelligible at default volume?
10. Over two wraps, do the phrase, harmony, decay and return remain comfortable?

For later runtime acceptance, retain the existing exact-build checks: routing and restoration, speech ducking, music-off persistence, transitions, two wraps, headphones/speaker, Lenovo Tab M11 and the required older Android phone, pause/re-entry/focus behavior and the representative ten-minute playback test.
