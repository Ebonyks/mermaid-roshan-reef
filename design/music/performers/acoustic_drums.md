## Creative identity
This performer makes the band feel connected. Their central skill is finding a small rhythmic idea that strengthens the tune, bass movement and scene identity, then developing it through touch, orchestration, repetition and well-placed silence.

They are a co-composer: they can originate a rhythmic hook, propose a change in phrase length, suggest where the chorus should arrive, or turn a melodic rhythm into a drum answer. Their function changes between groove leader, complementary player, foreground rhythmic voice, texture and rest. A percussionist, producer or bassist can carry an entire section while this drummer listens.

The current foundation is rounded lo-fi rhythm: soft or damped kick, controlled snare or rim, brush detail, restrained toms and useful space. Modern clarity remains valuable. Saturation, hiss and reduced bandwidth are optional production choices requiring a musical purpose and listening review.

Original *Donkey Kong Country* informs the larger arrangement: recognizable places, evolving instrumental landscapes, contrast and memorable returns. It does not require one kit, one genre or degraded audio throughout the score.

## Musical understanding
The performer should be able to hear and explain:

- The difference between the underlying pulse, subdivision, accent pattern and phrase.
- Straight eighths, sixteenth-note placement, swung subdivision, shuffle and compound-meter grouping. A brush sound does not automatically imply swing.
- How kick placement relates to bass attacks and releases. Some events reinforce one another; others create a conversation.
- Harmonic rhythm, cadences, pickups, phrase extensions and returns. The drummer follows these structural events without needing to strike every chord change.
- The distinction between rhythmic density and energy. A stronger chorus can come from clearer accents, longer resonance or a more confident repeating cell.
- Why a melodic phrase needs its ending. A fill that occupies the final breath can weaken both the melody and the section boundary.

They can follow a written form, read a practical drum chart, retain a recurring rhythmic hook and recognize where controlled variation is allowed. They should understand the selected Afro-Caribbean rhythmic framework through collaboration with the specialist percussionist; a generic “Latin” preset or swing control supplies neither that knowledge nor an appropriate part.

## Instrument range and technique
The proposed working range is a compact acoustic kit with interchangeable implements, selected rim/tom configurations and small percussion. Acoustic drums have a register and timbral range rather than a conventional melodic compass: low kick and floor tom, middle snare and higher toms, and brighter wire, wood and cymbal sounds.

| Setup | Proposed skills | Boundary to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| **Brush kit** | Continuous sweeps, lateral strokes, separate taps and accents, changing contact and pressure, coordination between sustained friction and discrete attacks. | A quiet stick pattern is not a complete brush performance. Repeating short brush hits cannot automatically represent continuous sweeping. |
| **Damped stick kit** | Controlled rebound, soft ghost notes, consistent principal accents, short kick envelopes, economical hi-hat playing and restrained fills. | Lowering the whole track does not create believable soft touch or recover an articulation absent from the samples. |
| **Rim and snare setup** | Distinguish cross-stick, rim-only clicks, head strokes and rimshots; select the intended attack and resonance. | These are separate gestures. A bright rimshot should not stand in for a dry cross-stick. Exact program support requires checking. |
| **Tom setup** | Short high/low replies, dynamic contours, playable sticking and intentional decay. | Avoid an automatic descending run at every boundary. Tom tuning and register should serve the phrase. |
| **Cymbals and hi-hat** | Closed/open contrast, foot closure, controlled sustain, sparse ride color and purposeful choking where supported. | Repeated crashes should not become the main way to announce a chorus. |
| **Small percussion** | A selected shaker, woodblock or similar auxiliary part, with credible motion and phrasing. | This does not confer conga, tabla, djembe or other specialist technique. Collaborate or declare another player. |

Hamilton’s published comments support studying brushes as a distinct instrumental practice and shaping drum phrases through register and dynamics. His own technique is a reference to investigate, rather than a claim that one grip or motion is mandatory for our fictional player. [Hamilton interview](https://www.slugmag.com/music/interviews/music-interviews/jiving-jazz-with-jeff-hamilton/).

## Pocket, touch and phrase construction
The starting task is to agree with the bassist and groove leader on what the listener should feel. Identify the anchor events, the answering events and the spaces that make the pattern recognizable.

Placement should be relational. A producer-led section can retain a firm electronic anchor while the drummer supplies a softer response. An acoustic-led section can use consistent differences between kick, snare and subdivision placement. Uniform random timing changes obscure that relationship; one global swing percentage cannot describe every limb or instrument.

Write the principal accent hierarchy first. Ghost notes should connect or prepare important events at a clearly lower dynamic level. Their presence must remain useful when the arrangement is heard quietly.

A drum phrase should have an opening, continuation and release. Possible development includes withholding an expected accent, transferring a response from snare to tom, thinning the subdivision, or resting before the returning hook. Choose the change because of the melody and form.

Fills have named jobs: announce a handoff, answer a completed phrase, prepare a return or conclude an episode. Each fill needs a clear landing and enough time to resume the groove. An exposed drum passage can be a complete written rhythmic statement, with its own memorable return.

## Improvisation and written-song discipline
Improvisation is a way to discover material during an authorized writing session. The selected song then records its hook, essential groove, section contrasts, entrances, withdrawals and ending.

The performer can vary touch and small connecting details within declared windows. They preserve the identifying rhythmic cell and approved melodies. Any proposed melodic or formal change is brought back to the composition brief.

Full pieces need written development: verse/refrain organization or another purposeful structure such as atmospheric introduction, lyrical A, contrasting B, changed return and coda. The kit must help those sections sound different. A short diagnostic beat is evidence about a sound source, not a finished arrangement.

## Collaboration
| Partner | Working agreement |
|---|---|
| **Bass** | Decide which attacks coincide, where one player answers, and how their note lengths leave room. Preserve passages where the bass figure leads. |
| **Producer** | Name one groove leader per section. Agree on timing, kick ownership and the purpose of any electronic/acoustic combination. Keep original performance and processed versions identifiable. |
| **Afro-Caribbean percussionist** | Establish the chosen idiom and rhythmic cycle together. Allocate low-drum weight, timekeeping and fills so both parts remain audible. |
| **Traditional/world percussionist** | Let the selected drum’s articulated phrase shape the arrangement. The acoustic kit may contribute a small response or rest entirely. |
| **Melody and harmony players** | Learn their phrase endings and releases. Use punctuation that prepares or answers them, with space around the hook. |

One practical writing order is: hear the hook and bass idea; agree on the groove leader; choose the smallest useful kit; mark section changes and rests; then add only the detail the song needs.

## Physical performance and overdubs
A single kit performance must fit two hands and two feet. Two simultaneous hand strokes plus kick and a hi-hat-foot event can be credible; adding a shaker hand or another snare stroke requires a different allocation, another player or an overdub.

Continuous brush contact occupies a hand. Moving that hand to a tom, cymbal or auxiliary instrument changes or interrupts the sweep. Brush-to-stick changes need a practical gap or a declared separate take. Cross-stick, rimshot and sweeping gestures cannot be treated as interchangeable simultaneous sounds from one hand.

Chart difficult events with hand/foot assignments and allow movement between surfaces. Check fills at the actual tempo, including the return to the timekeeping position.

Studio overdubs are available when they improve the song. Label the performer, instrument, take and musical purpose. A second shaker pass should remain a deliberate part rather than an invisible extra limb.

## Tone and listening priorities
Preserve audible drum body, soft attacks and useful differences between accents and connecting notes. Let damping control excess resonance while retaining character. Keep cymbal sustain proportionate to the melody, speech and room.

Begin with performance and balance. Apply EQ to a demonstrated masking or tonal problem. Preserve a clear pulse on phone speakers without relying on deep kick energy, exaggerated clicks or stereo width. Check percussion transients around spoken instructions and touch feedback.

The existing Gemini advisory for `lofi_dreams_jazz_kit` raises possible low-mid accumulation and rim-edge emphasis. Those remain listening hypotheses. Its suggestion that clarity limits lo-fi suitability does not establish a defect or require added saturation. No subjective result closes human acceptance, and no new audio was uploaded in this profile pass.

## Failure modes and acceptance questions
| Failure | Acceptance question |
|---|---|
| Generic loop underneath a changing song | Does the kit help the listener recognize the verse, contrast and return? |
| Constant filling | Can the melodic phrase finish clearly? Does every fill have a named job? |
| Indiscriminate timing variation | Is there a stable, repeatable relationship between anchor and answering events? |
| Brush preset used as quiet sticks | Are the required friction, sweep and attack gestures actually supported and reviewed? |
| Competing acoustic and electronic kits | Who leads this section, and what unique contribution does each part make? |
| Specialist percussion reduced to decoration | Can its phrase lead, answer or shape the form without the kit covering it? |
| Impossible limb use | Can the chart be performed with the declared players and overdubs? |
| Artificial lo-fi treatment | Does processing improve the scene while preserving touch, hook and intelligibility? |
| Technical pass mistaken for musical approval | Are human, owner, child and device results independently recorded? |
