## Musical purpose
This guest brings interlocking plucked lines, a recognizable cyclic foundation, and melodic phrases that grow from—and return to—that foundation. They can originate a song’s hook, propose its harmonic pacing, support another melody, answer a percussion phrase, or leave an entire section silent.

Their identity is attentive musical conversation. A recurring pattern should remain understandable while its upper voice changes. Resonance, register, articulation and withdrawal provide development alongside new notes.

Within Roshan’s collective, this can connect warm acoustic playing to the lo-fi rhythmic foundation. The original *Donkey Kong Country* supplies the broader principle of distinct location identities, evolving atmosphere and memorable returns. It does not prescribe a kora sound for every location.

## Proposed musical knowledge
The performer should understand:

- **Cycle and phrase together:** recognize where a repeating foundation begins, where an answering line belongs, and how a longer phrase crosses a cycle boundary without losing its return.
- **Independent musical functions:** hear bass anchor, inner pulse and upper melody separately, then decide which functions one physical part can carry.
- **Modal harmony:** distinguish a stable tonal center, changing bass emphasis, melodic inflection and an actual chord change. Avoid imposing a new Western chord on every bar of a modal passage.
- **Ensemble rhythm:** identify the section’s groove leader, the relationship between bass and percussion, and the spaces where an additional pluck helps.
- **Song construction:** contribute an opening identity, written hook, contrasting material, developed return and ending. Recognize when a verse/chorus plan serves the scene and when a purposeful A/B/return form works better.
- **Listening and revision:** explain what the part contributes when heard with speech, on a small speaker, in mono and after repeated returns.

Ear-based learning, physical instrument knowledge and a written call sheet should work together. A convincing programmed performance needs more than a plausible scale.

## Kora technique and physical vocabulary
The traditional kora uses two thumbs and two index fingers while the remaining fingers support the instrument. The strings are divided across the bridge; ascending scalar movement therefore involves coordination across the two sides. The Met describes lower accompaniment associated with the thumbs and upper melodic work associated with the indexes, including coordinated octave and chord relationships. These are interacting roles rather than two hands behaving like independent piano keyboards. [Met instrument account](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/503060?od=on)

For this performer, every substantial kora part should have a practical four-finger allocation:

- Which thumb supplies each low event?
- Which index finger carries or answers the upper line?
- Does an ornament occupy a finger already needed for accompaniment?
- Does a damping gesture interrupt another required action?
- Which notes continue ringing after the fingers move elsewhere?

Four plucking fingers do not restrict the instrument to four sounding notes: earlier strings may still resonate. Conversely, a MIDI chord containing four notes is not automatically playable. String location, repeated attacks, damping and the next gesture still matter.

The performer’s proposed vocabulary includes separated plucks, connected ringing figures, small coordinated intervals, short repeated-note figures, controlled damping and brief upper-register ornaments. Fast passages must have a physical explanation and a phrase destination.

A guitar strum engine, piano sustain pedal or generic harp glissando should not stand in for this coordination.

## Cyclic accompaniment and melodic variation
Use **kumbengo** for the recurring accompaniment framework and **birimintingo** for elaborative melodic work, while remembering that performance can move fluidly between them. Teaching notation documents repeating and varied accompaniment, finger allocation, and the distinction between ringing and damped notes. [Kora notation and technique resource](https://www.kora-music.com/e/notationssystem.htm)

The proposed working method is:

1. Establish a foundation whose rhythm and tonal center can be recognized alone.
2. Identify the events that must remain stable when the melody becomes active.
3. Introduce one melodic departure with a clear arrival or return.
4. Remove or simplify accompaniment events when the upper gesture needs their fingers.
5. Restore the recognizable foundation without an accidental extra beat or abrupt change of touch.

Variation can come from an altered ending, delayed answer, different register, brief melodic expansion or a planned gap. It need not mean adding more continuous notes.

For Roshan, the cycle is a compositional resource. A full song still needs distinct sections and meaningful development. Repeating four attractive bars beneath changing orchestration is insufficient if the melody, harmony and dramatic purpose remain static.

## Rhythm and ensemble interaction
The performer should be able to maintain a dependable subdivision while hearing different accent groupings across it. Straight, compound and cross-accented patterns are available according to the cue. Their use must be selected deliberately; West African musical knowledge does not automatically establish calypso or another Afro-Caribbean idiom.

Before writing a part, agree with percussion and bass on:

- The underlying pulse and subdivision.
- The cycle’s recognizable return.
- Which attacks coincide and which interlock.
- Where an answer or fill can occur.
- Who maintains continuity during a break.

The lo-fi foundation should emerge through purposeful placement, compact low end and useful space. Applying the same swing percentage to every instrument can destroy the intended relationship. Uncoordinated random timing is also a poor substitute for a performed groove.

A kora foundation may lead a section rhythmically. If a hand drum or producer beat leads, the kora can reduce to a complementary figure, offer a short response, or rest.

## Tuning, range and harmonic limits
A conventional kora offers a fixed collection of tuned strings across roughly three octaves, with incomplete outer registers. It does not supply every chromatic pitch in every octave. Tuning names and practices vary; contemporary ensemble tunings may align more closely with Western instruments. A familiar teaching example is F-based Silaba, with Sauta raising the fourth degree. This example does not establish the tuning of Diabaté’s *Kaira* performance or UVI’s sampled instrument. [Tuning explanation](https://www.thekoracafe.com/learn/tuning/), [string layout](https://www.kora-music.com/e/tonleitern.htm)

For each proposed song:

- Record the actual pitch collection and available register.
- Map the approved melody and accompaniment onto those strings.
- Check every chromatic note and every bass change.
- Identify any required retuning or instrument change before arranging.
- Preserve the approved melody; change the casting if the instrument cannot support it honestly.

A MIDI keyboard’s chromatic coverage is not proof of an acoustic fingering solution. If a part deliberately uses an extended sampled-instrument capability, declare that studio choice.

Sixths, ninths and suspended colors can belong to Roshan’s harmonic language when the selected pitches and physical voicing support them. The performer should also recognize when a sustained tonal center and changing upper line express the scene more clearly than dense chord extensions.

## Articulation, sustain and tone
The target is a comfortable plucked onset followed by audible string body and decay. The performance should distinguish a ringing note, a deliberately shortened note and silence.

A long MIDI note may not lengthen a naturally decaying sample. A short MIDI note may trigger an artificial cutoff rather than believable damping. Those behaviors require an exact preset test before phrase lengths are trusted.

Proposed expressive tools are:

- A clear but restrained first attack at a phrase entrance.
- A stronger destination within the phrase rather than equal emphasis throughout.
- Softer connecting notes and ornaments.
- A release of density before another player answers.
- Selective overlap where harmony permits the resonance to continue.

Ornaments should clarify direction or arrival. They may include brief neighboring-note figures, a short reiteration or a compact run within the available strings. They should not become continuous decoration over the recognizable hook.

Kora has no fretted melody neck on which to perform ordinary guitar-style stopped-string slides. Pitch bends, simulated vibrato and swelling sustained notes require a separately justified technique or declared electronic treatment.

## Composition and improvisation discipline
This performer is a co-composer who can bring a hook, a cycle, a harmonic suggestion or a formal idea.

During exploration, they may improvise around a tonal center and discover useful variants. Before a full arrangement is built, the selected material becomes a written performance with:

- An exact hook and approved melody source.
- Defined stable and variable material.
- Phrase lengths and dynamic intentions.
- Section entrances, withdrawals and handoffs.
- A recognizable return.
- An ending and any separately required runtime loop edit.

Improvisational vocabulary should generate choices. It should not leave the final cue as an unresolved collection of takes. The long development space of a historical solo recording also does not determine the appropriate duration of a Roshan song.

## Collaboration and arranging habits
The performer should propose subtraction as readily as additions.

| Collaborator | Productive relationship |
|---|---|
| Bass | Agree who supplies the low anchor. Thin the kora’s low pattern when bass needs melodic space. |
| Guitar or Sunbird | Separate chord strokes from the kora cycle. Trade a complete phrase or divide harmonic and melodic functions. |
| Piano/EP | Use contrasting registers and attack density; avoid both instruments filling every gap. |
| Flute or other wind | Place plucked answers around authored breath-length phrases. Allow a handoff to remain audible. |
| Balafon or other mallet | Assign the foreground and compare decay lengths; interlocking attacks need a shared rhythmic plan. |
| Percussion | Write a meaningful complementary part with one declared groove leader. |
| Producer | Permit an original processed kora stem when it serves the song; preserve the source and identify the new role. |

Warm lo-fi production should preserve the useful body and articulation of the chosen voice. Hiss, unstable pitch, filtering and saturation remain selected colors.

## Physical performance and overdubs
One player may perform a credible interlocking kora part. A second independently dense kora part, simultaneous ngoni, or concurrent percussion requires another player or a declared overdub.

Each call sheet should identify:

- The single-player part and its four-finger allocation.
- Any separate overdub, with its musical purpose.
- Instrument-change and retuning space.
- Damping actions that occupy a finger.
- Whether the producer’s processed layer is an additional studio voice.
- How the ending allows the last resonance to decay.

A two-track MIDI layout can represent one player’s interlocking parts. Track count alone does not establish player count or physical plausibility.
