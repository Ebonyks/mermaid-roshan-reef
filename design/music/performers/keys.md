## Musical identity

`keys` is a fictional keyboard player and co-composer whose contribution is **clear harmonic direction, a memorable melodic sentence, responsive rhythm, and carefully shaped space**.

This member can originate a hook, suggest a bass movement, clarify a chorus arrival, or reshape an ensemble’s density. Their instrument and role change with the scene: acoustic piano for a direct, intimate statement; electric piano for a rounded pulse or lyrical response; organ for connected harmony and expressive sustain; synth for a deliberately electronic melodic or atmospheric voice.

These are proposed capabilities. The historical artists below are study references, not project participants, and their recordings do not establish that our software instruments can reproduce their touch.

The player’s recurring habits should be recognizable across instruments:

- Make the foreground easy to follow.
- Voice chords so their movement remains audible.
- Respond to another player’s phrase before adding independent activity.
- Use note length, register, dynamics, and silence to distinguish sections.
- Return to the written hook reliably after development.
- Let another collaborator’s idea determine the arrangement when it serves the song.

## Musical knowledge and arranging competence

The proposed player should understand harmony well enough to choose **what the listener needs to hear**, rather than continually demonstrating harmonic vocabulary.

## Voicing and harmonic direction
- Know chord inversions, shell voicings, thirds and sevenths as guide tones where appropriate, and smooth movement between inner voices.
- Distinguish the written melody from a chord’s convenient top note. Revoice accompaniment when it obscures or contradicts the tune.
- Use sixths, added ninths, suspensions, and open spacing selectively. Their function must be audible: warmth, anticipation, openness, or arrival.
- Recognize when modal harmony or a pedal tone needs patience. A discovery cue does not automatically need a functional dominant cadence.
- Omit redundant roots when the bass player already establishes them. Avoid dense low-register chords that blur bass identity.
- Coordinate with guitar, harp, and plucked instruments so the ensemble has one coherent harmonic rhythm.
- Treat reharmonization as a proposal requiring review. Preserve approved melodies and their recognizable character.

## Melody and bass independence
The player can carry a melody above a quieter accompaniment, hold an inner voice while another moves, and maintain a simple bass figure beneath an independent phrase. Each layer needs its own dynamics and articulation.

When a bassist is active, the left hand usually moves upward, simplifies, or rests. Keyboard bass becomes an explicit arrangement choice for a duo, break, transition, or featured passage. It must hand responsibility back cleanly.

## Rhythm
The player understands straight subdivisions, swing, compound meter, and syncopation as different organizing choices. They can place a short chord before a beat, release across a percussion answer, or sustain through an active groove without filling every subdivision.

A Caribbean-informed part requires a named rhythmic context and interaction with the ensemble. Generic offbeat chords do not establish a complete idiom. Likewise, a lo-fi foundation needs intentional placement and a stable shared pulse; random timing offsets do not supply feel.

## Phrase construction, dynamics, and improvisation

A keyboard phrase needs an intention, a point of emphasis, and a release. A useful default is a clear statement, a shorter answer, breathing room, and a recognizable return. Phrase length follows the melody and scene.

The player should be able to:

- Bring the melody above accompaniment through touch and spacing.
- Build toward an arrival without making every note louder.
- Let supporting parts recede when the foreground enters.
- Change a return through voicing, register, instrumentation, or dynamics while retaining the approved hook.
- Leave complete phrases of space when another member has something meaningful to say.
- Finish an answer early enough for the next entrance to read.

**Improvisation belongs inside a written agreement.** Exploration can discover a response, voicing, rhythmic placement, or transition. The selected result is then documented and repeatable. Specify where ornaments may vary, where the hook remains exact, and where the player must rest.

A full piece needs a reviewed form: verse/refrain/bridge, or a purposefully defined atmospheric equivalent such as statement/landscape/changed return/coda. A sequence of chord patches is insufficient. Musical contrast should come from harmony, phrase, groove, register, and changing responsibility across the band.

## Collaboration and physical limits

The keyboard member brings ideas to the collective without becoming its automatic harmonic authority.

- **With strings:** agree which instrument supplies chord body and which answers. Avoid continuous matching chord attacks.
- **With bass:** agree on roots, inversions, pickups, rests, and the exact bass handoff.
- **With percussion:** identify one groove leader per section. Keys can reinforce a rhythmic identity or leave room for a pitched-stroke reply.
- **With winds and pan:** respect phrase endings and resonance. Enter after a meaningful gap rather than answering every note.
- **With the producer:** decide who owns a synth part or processed keyboard response. Preserve the original performance separately from edits.

A proposed one-player part starts from two hands and credible movement. Compact voicings are preferred; wide spans, rapid register jumps, and repeated chords need a playability review. Sustain pedal does not create additional hands.

Simultaneous independent piano, EP, organ, and synth parts require a declared split/layer setup, another performer, or studio overdubs. A layered patch remains one gesture unless separate performances are recorded. Drawbar changes, modulation-wheel gestures, and other manual controls also consume attention or a hand; document automation and overdubs honestly.

## Planning examples — no new composition

### Opera

**Identity:** exactly one principal steel pan.

Possible form: introduction → character verse → pan refrain → contrasting response section → returning refrain → ending.

Keys can begin with sparse EP harmony, provide short verse answers, and reduce to guide tones or rest under the pan refrain. A bridge may give keys a complete phrase drawn from reviewed material, followed by a clear release that returns the foreground to pan.

Select the groove leader separately. Guitar and keys divide harmonic attacks; bass owns the low foundation. Avoid a bright keyboard doubling that creates a competing pan-like signature. Smokey has native comp evidence, but its Opera role still requires listening and a revised cue plan.

### Sky Lagoon

**Identity:** non-pan; preserve the flute relationship with valiha and udu.

Possible form: opening space → flute statement → contrasting instrumental landscape → changed flute return → coda.

Keys may enter after the initial identity is established, supplying slow harmonic movement or a sparse response. A middle passage could feature a reviewed keyboard statement while flute rests. Keys then thin or withdraw before the flute return.

Let udu or another specifically chosen rhythm voice lead the pulse. Keep valiha transients and flute phrase endings clear. EP is a casting proposal here; organ and soft synth remain unauditioned candidates.

### Castle

**Identity:** non-pan; warm Sunbird-led home music.

Possible form: opening recognition → guitar verse → homecoming refrain → keyboard bridge/response → returning refrain → ending.

The Nordic upright could become a distinct bridge or response voice while Sunbird rests or moves to simple support. The unchanged melody comparison makes this worth reviewing. It does not authorize replacing the established guitar lead.

A single keyboard player can switch from EP to upright between sections. If their independent parts overlap, declare an overdub. Bass and a restrained groove carry continuity through the handoff. The returning refrain must remain recognizable without relying on greater loudness.

## Failure modes and acceptance questions

| Failure | Review question or corrective direction |
|---|---|
| Chords obscure the tune | Can the foreground be followed at low volume? Reduce inner voices, change register, or shorten accompaniment. |
| Left hand duplicates bass | Is the duplication intentional? Assign low-register ownership and simplify the other part. |
| Continuous decorative playing | Does each response have a purpose? Remove fills and preserve meaningful silence. |
| “Humanization” weakens the groove | Do timing choices reinforce the shared pulse and percussion conversation? |
| Piano articulation is transferred unchanged to organ | Are expression, overlap, registration, and releases written for the organ? |
| Lo-fi processing removes note identity | Does the hook remain clear in mono and beneath speech? Review the source and effect balance. |
| Instrument changes substitute for development | Do sections differ in musical purpose, phrasing, harmony, or rhythm? |
| One velocity test is called expressive | Have the required dynamics and transitions actually been demonstrated? |
| Upright proposal becomes assumed acceptance | Has the exact candidate received contextual human and owner review? |
| Keyboard layers imply impossible performance | Are splits, automation, additional performers, and overdubs declared? |

Final review asks whether the hook returns clearly, each section has an identifiable foreground, percussion remains meaningful, and handoffs create space. Human review must also address fatigue, harshness, speech masking, mono balance, and the intended device.
