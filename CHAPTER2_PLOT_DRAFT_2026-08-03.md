# CHAPTER 2 — "ROSHAN MAKES HER OWN BIRTHDAY"
## Rough draft of the plot, under the settled terms of 2026-08-03

*Read-aloud treatment. Spoken lines are quoted and are meant to be said out loud. Audience is one four-year-old non-reader: no fail states, nobody is beaten, driven off, or made sad, and the lesson is never stated — only enacted.*

---

## 1. COLD OPEN — "It's My Birthday and I'm Making It"

The castle is clean. She cleaned it yesterday.

Morning comes into the royal bedroom the way it does after a good sleep, and Roshan wakes up in the big castle bed with her hair completely sideways. She remembers before she is even all the way awake, and she says it to the ceiling:

> **Roshan:** "It's my birthday."

Princess Huluu is already at the door, because Huluu is the kind of friend who is already at the door. She has a list. She has, in fact, had the list since last night.

> **Huluu:** "Happy birthday! Sit down. Don't move. I will have the whole party MADE for you — the cake, the music, the decorations, everything. You won't have to lift a finger."
>
> **Roshan:** "No thank you."
>
> **Huluu:** "...No thank you?"
>
> **Roshan:** "I want to make it. That's the fun part. That's the part I *want*."

Huluu looks at her list. Huluu puts the list in her pocket. Huluu decides, on the spot, to be the best helper anybody ever had.

They go down to the **Main Hall**, and there it is: the long table, swept and empty, with nothing on it at all. A whole table of nothing. Roshan puts both hands flat on it.

> **Roshan:** "Everything my party needs is going to go right here. And I'm going to make all of it."

And then — while she is standing there with her hands on the empty table — something moves at the high window above the hall.

**No line. No music sting. No fright.** Just a boy about her size, up on the sill with the light behind him. He has a small crown of little flames, the way another child might have a paper hat. He is watching her table. He is not impressed and he is not angry; he is doing the thing children do when they are looking at something they have never been given. He is there for three seconds. Then the sill is empty and there is one thin curl of warm smoke going up, and it smells faintly like a birthday candle just blown out.

Roshan doesn't see it. The child watching does.

Down the corridor, out of sight, somebody giggles.

> **Imp Captain (offstage):** "A party? Ooh. *We* love parties."

---

## 2. ACT ONE — THE INVITATIONS (Mermaid Pool / Astronaut Engineer, with Mewsha)

*This act is written long because it teaches the child the shape of every act that follows.*

Roshan starts where a party actually starts, which is not with cake.

> **Roshan:** "If nobody knows, nobody comes. So first — everybody has to be TOLD."

Down in the **Mermaid Pool**, **Mewsha** the cat is already in her fishbowl helmet, sitting beside the bubble rocket with her tail wrapped around her feet, meowing once, politely.

**The scuffle.** But the pool is a mess. Four **plain mischief imps** — small, purple, no costumes, pure weather — are in the tubes. One is drinking out of the pipe. One has the valve wheel and is spinning it like a toy. One is wearing the funnel. They aren't wicked; they are four-year-olds with no supervision.

> **Imp:** "Is that for the PARTY? Let me HOLD it!"

Roshan sees them off — bop, bop, bop — and they scatter, giggling, delighted to have been noticed at all. Nobody is hurt. Nobody is sad. One of them waves.

**The arrival.** The pool is quiet. And *then* a figure walks in from the side, deliberately, taking his time so the child can look at him properly: an imp in **exactly Roshan's astronaut suit**, exactly her helmet, exactly her boots. He stands next to her. He copies her stance. He announces himself like a boy announcing a school project:

> **Astronaut Imp:** "I was sent to learn the ROCKET. I am learning it. Don't mind me."

He does not attack her. He gets a clipboard.

**The job.** She makes the invitations — she draws them, one for each friend — and then the five beats of the launch, with the imp doing his homework badly one station behind her the whole time. She routes the **bubble tubes**; he routes his backwards and gets a faceful. She **patches** the leaks; he patches his own glove to the pipe. She turns the **valve**; he turns his the wrong way and rises slowly to the ceiling and has to be got down. She builds the **boost**; his fizzles. Mewsha watches him with the flat, level stare of a cat.

**The count.** Before the launch, the important thing: Roshan counts her friends on her fingers, twice around.

> **Roshan:** "Huluu. Evie. Wacky. Kareem. Rosalina. Harper. Fiona. Chuck. Sparkle. Nurse Faron. Flower Friend. And Daddy." *(She looks at the last invitation, then at Mewsha.)* "This one I'm giving you myself."

**Twelve.** The child hears the number and *sees* the twelve invitations go in. This number is the spine of the whole chapter — twelve bags, twelve helpings, a cake cut twelve ways, a hall dressed big enough for twelve — and every job after this one is measured against it.

**LAUNCH.** The bubble rocket goes up through the water and bursts into twelve smaller bubbles, and each one carries an invitation off in a different direction, and each one is *named out loud as it goes*. The child watches her party leave the castle and go find people.

**The steal.** In the quiet afterwards, the astronaut imp does the thing he was always going to do. He cannot make one. So he takes hers — the finished rocket, the last bright bubble — and he runs for the stage.

> **Imp Captain (appearing, bowing):** "Hee hee! You'll have to bop ME twice!"

**The finale.** She catches him on the stage doing her job, in her suit, in front of everybody, and she out-flies him — not by hurting him, by being *better at it*, which is the only way anybody wins anything in this book. He loses gracefully, bows, and says his one small true thing:

> **Astronaut Imp:** "I only wanted to see where it goes. Nobody ever sent me anywhere."

Then she carries the rocket back to the **Main Hall**, and it lands on the empty table, and it is the first thing on it, and the table is a little less empty.

**And the rule of the chapter is now established, without a word of explanation:** the little ones make a mess, she sees them off, the one in her costume arrives to *learn*, copies it wrong, takes it, loses it back, and the thing goes on the table.

---

## 3. THE MIDDLE — Eleven More Things a Party Needs

Between every room, **the Racer** carries her. **Harper and Fiona** wait at each doorway with the shell-kart, one holding the flag, one already revving:

> **Harper:** "Where to next, birthday girl?"

Short, fast, no stakes, no prize — the racer is not making anything for the table, he is *how she gets there*, and by the end of the day the child understands that job perfectly. (This is the seed of Chapter 3, when getting there is the whole story.)

### ROYAL KITCHEN — the cake, and the bags

**PASTRY CHEF (Kareem).** She walks into Kareem's cake shop corner of the kitchen on her own birthday and tells him she is not buying one. A boxed shop cake sits on the cart all act long, perfect and untouched, the road not taken. She bakes hers beside his — big enough to cut **twelve** ways. The **chef imp** arrives in the toque, on backwards, and whisks a rock in an empty bowl with total conviction. He takes the cake and loses it back, and admits at the finale that he is just *hungry*, and Kareem gives him a bun.

**CANDY MAKER (Sparkle).** Twelve party bags, one for every friend — except Sparkle the baby eagle keeps eating them, chirp, chirp, gone. Roshan works out why: nobody had made **Sparkle** one. So she makes Sparkle's first, and then she makes **extra ones**, more than twelve, just in case somebody comes who wasn't counted. *(This is a plant. Do not explain it. It pays off in section 5.)* The **candy imp** eats the evidence twice and is caught with his cheeks full.

### FAMILY DINING ROOM — the picnic

**FARMER (Chuck).** Twelve helpings, because twelve are coming, and the animals eat too — Chuck the dog says all of it in barks and whimpers he has already recorded, and not one new sound. Twelve piggies to feed and one of them, **Pudding**, is wearing a party hat, so the child can find her in a second. Pudding is a **guest**, never food. The **farmer imp** herds the piggies enthusiastically in the wrong direction and ends up under all of them. His want, at the finale: he has never been allowed to *hold* one. Chuck lets him hold Pudding.

### ROYAL LIBRARY — the crown, and the first clue

**DETECTIVE (Huluu).** Huluu takes off her own tiara and puts it on Roshan's head as a birthday crown — *"so everybody knows whose day it is"* — and the moment it leaves her hair it is gone. The search through the shelves is the act. And in the shelves Roshan finds something she was not looking for: **one of her twelve invitations, come back**, with a burnt corner and a small handprint on it.

> **Huluu:** "That one isn't ours. Nobody we know is that warm."

She keeps it in her pocket. She does not solve it. *(Second plant.)* The **detective imp**, in the deerstalker, is discovered hiding badly behind a book he is holding upside down, and confesses at the finale that he has never once been *looked for*.

### CRAFT ROOM — the hall itself

**PAINTER (Flower Friend).** This is not a picture in a frame. This is **making the room look like a party** — banners, streamers, the whole Main Hall turned from a room into an event, and it has to be dressed big enough for twelve. Flower Friend never speaks; she poses, and the colour is how she is present. The **painter imp** paints beautifully and entirely in one colour, blue, everything blue, because blue is the only one he likes.

**AND HERE THE COPY GETS GOOD.** On the way out of the craft room, through a door left ajar, Roshan sees it for the first time: a **packing crate with a paper tablecloth on it**, and on the crate — a lumpy grey cake, a bag of pebbles, a chalk drawing of a banner. A little table with a little party laid on it, wrong in every way. It is not funny. She looks at it for a moment longer than she means to, and moves on.

### THE COURTYARD — first ember henchmen

Harper and Fiona drive her out through the courtyard and stop hard, because the gate is blocked. These are **not** the purple ones. These are **fiery ember imps** — four of them, in a ring, hissing like kettles, made of campfire, sent from somewhere else entirely.

> **Imp Captain (quietly, for the first time not laughing):** "...Those aren't ours."

She sees them off — bop, puff, a curl of smoke, and four small sooty imps sitting down hard on the flagstones looking annoyed. She gives them a cup of water each. They drink it. They stay sitting there for the rest of the day, watching. Nobody is defeated. But the child now knows: **somebody else is involved.**

### STUFFIE PLAYROOM — the mended guest, and the games

**STUFFIE SURGEON (Evie, and Lamba).** Lamba is torn. Evie stands at the edge of the operating table with her hands over her mouth and does not move the entire act, and that is the tension meter — the child watches Evie's shoulders, not the clock. Lamba is mended, because **a guest who is broken still gets to come.** The **doctor imp** has something torn of his own, held behind his back the whole act, and at the finale he finally shows it, and Roshan sews it for him.

**BOXER (Wacky).** The champion's belt gets re-tailored into a birthday sash by Grandpa Wacky, who corner-coaches with a threaded needle in his teeth. This is the party's **wild half** — the running-around games for the big kids who won't dance. The **boxer imp** is all noise and no guard; what he wants, it turns out, is somebody in his corner. Wacky stands in it for him for one round.

### BUBBLE BATH — the soft corner

**NURSERY NURSE (Faron).** Twelve guests means little ones, and little ones need somewhere soft *at* the party, not away from it. The act is the one thing Roshan has never been good at: **being quiet.** Faron is beside her from the first beat. The **nursery imp** is, embarrassingly, genuinely gentle — he rocks the baby properly, correctly, tenderly, and then notices he is being watched and pretends he wasn't.

**And the copy is now a working party.** Passing the crate table again, it has real things on it. Her things. A real ribbon. A real bag. A real painted banner, folded wrong. It is beginning to look, from a distance, like a party.

### OPERA HALL — the three performances

**BALLERINA (Rosalina).** A music box that only plays while somebody is dancing, and Rosalina teaches her the dance she will do at her own party, and Huluu — a princess with nothing to do all evening otherwise — learns it too. The **ballerina imp** can, it turns out, actually dance. Beautifully. And nobody has ever watched him do it. She watches. That is his whole prize.

**POP STAR (Daddy Mermaid).** Daddy sings, using only the recordings that already exist, not one new sound. Roshan finds out what a microphone is actually *for* the moment the imps unplug it and the back of the hall can't hear — **everybody has to hear, even the ones at the back.** The **pop star imp** knows exactly one line of one song and sings it fourteen times.

**Second ember henchmen — the Main Hall doorway.** Just as the hall is being dressed for the evening, **six ember imps** come up the steps in a spiral and go for the lights. This is bigger, faster, and warmer-coloured than the courtyard, and it is over quickly, and afterwards there are six more small sooty imps sitting down looking cross, and Roshan gives them water too. But the hall smells of smoke now, and the child knows the day has somebody else's fingerprints on it.

**MAGICIAN (Evie + Lamba) — the last piece.** Evie watches from the stage lip with her hands over her mouth again, and Lamba is the vanishing subject, and the trick is the thing to *watch* for everybody who doesn't want to dance. And the **magician imp** — the one who has wanted, this entire chapter, simply to be **watched** — does his version so nearly perfectly that for one second she genuinely thinks he has done it.

Then he takes the hat and runs, and she catches him, and she wins it back, and when she lifts the hat on stage, out comes **Lamba wearing a tiny imp party hat**.

Which is not a thing Roshan put there.

---

## 4. THE TURN — The Other Table

She follows the magician imp backstage with the hat in her hands, and finds where they have been going all day.

The **crate with the paper tablecloth**, and on it, laid out with enormous care: a cake made of sand with sticks in it. A bag of pebbles tied with a real ribbon. A banner painted entirely blue. A crown cut out of paper. And propped at the head of the little table, drawn in chalk on the crate itself, **a boy with a crown of flames**, sitting at a party, with everyone facing him.

Twelve imps in twelve costumes stand around it, holding her things, absolutely still, caught.

Roshan does not shout and does not take anything back. She looks at the chalk drawing for a long moment.

> **Roshan:** "...You weren't stealing it. You were *practising*."

She leaves the crate table exactly as it is. She goes to the Main Hall. She puts the magician's hat down on the party table, and the table is **full** — every single thing a party needs, made by her, sitting in a row.

Outside the windows the day has gone gold and then orange. The lamps come on. The doors open, and the guests come in, all twelve of them, named and expected. Huluu straightens the birthday crown on Roshan's head. Someone starts the music box, so someone has to dance.

And from the doorway, small, not coming in:

> **Imp Captain:** "...Are we invited?"

Everything stops. Twelve imps in twelve costumes are standing in the doorway holding their paper crown.

> **Roshan:** "Imps — you're invited."

Nobody explains what just happened. Nobody says the word *sharing*. The Captain takes off the paper crown, and they come in.

*(And the extra party bags — the ones she made because of Sparkle, the ones nobody counted — are exactly enough.)*

---

## 5. THE PARTY

The Main Hall, full, warm, loud.

**Kareem** cuts the cake twelve ways and then, without a word, cuts it smaller so it goes further. **Evie** gets Lamba a seat right next to the cake, which is all Lamba ever wanted, and Lamba's mend doesn't show at all. **Chuck** does not reach the cake first, because the farmer did his job — he has his own dinner, and **Pudding** is at the table in her party hat as a guest, being fed grapes by the farmer imp, who is having the best evening of his life.

**Rosalina** and **Huluu** and Roshan do the dance from the music box, and the box plays exactly as long as they keep dancing. **Wacky** runs the wild games at the far end for everyone who won't dance, with the boxer imp in his corner. **Daddy** sings, and because of the microphone, the back of the hall hears every word. The **magician imp** does his trick — and this time the whole room is watching him on purpose, and he gets it right, and he has to sit down afterwards.

**Nurse Faron** has the soft corner set up by the wall, and the littlest guests are asleep in it *at* the party, not away from it, with the nursery imp rocking one of them and looking embarrassed about it. **Flower Friend** doesn't say anything all evening; the hall is what she says. **Sparkle** gets her party bag first and does not eat anybody else's. **Mewsha** sits in the exact middle of the table, because she is a cat.

Out through the window, on the courtyard flagstones, ten small sooty ember imps sit and watch the lights and do not come in.

Then the lamps go down, and Kareem carries in the cake with **five candles** on it, and the whole hall — friends, family, twelve imps in twelve costumes — sings.

Roshan closes her eyes to make her wish.

---

## 6. THE CRASH

The doors do not bang open. That would be frightening, and this book is never frightening.

Instead the whole hall goes warm. The light bends orange, the way it does near a fire, and every flame in the room leans the same direction, and the singing trails off because everyone can feel it on their faces.

He is standing at the end of the long table: a boy exactly Roshan's size, arms folded, in a crown of small flames. The same boy from the window in the morning. The same boy drawn in chalk on the crate.

The Imp Captain drops his party bag.

> **Imp Captain:** "That's... that's our Prince."

The **Ember Prince** walks the whole length of the table, and he doesn't smash one thing on it. He looks at each piece as he passes it — the rocket, the bags, the sash, the hat — with an expression the child will recognise instantly, because it is the face you make at somebody else's presents.

> **Ember Prince:** "You made all this. By yourself."
>
> **Roshan:** "With my friends."
>
> **Ember Prince:** "I've never had one. Not one. My father doesn't do birthdays." *(a beat)* "So I'll take the part I liked best."

And he leans over the cake and blows out the candles — **one at a time**, counting them off, *one, two, three, four, five* — and each one that goes out, he takes, until the five candles are in his fist and the hall is dark except for him.

> **Ember Prince:** "They're at my father's mountain now. At the fire mountain, past the north, with the five lanterns." *(at the door, not unkindly, which is worse)* "If you want them — come and get them."

And he is gone, and the lamps come back on, and nothing else in the whole room has been touched. The cake is fine. The bags are fine. Everyone is fine. There is just a cake with five little holes in it and no candles, and no wish.

Roshan takes the burnt-cornered invitation out of her pocket, the one that came back, and looks at the small handprint on it.

> **Roshan:** "He got one. It came back."
>
> **Huluu:** "Somebody sent it back for him."
>
> **Rosalina:** "The old folk in the far north knew his father. They'll know the way."
>
> **Imp Captain (very small):** "Our cousins live at his gate."

Roshan looks down the table at everything she made today, and then at the empty middle of the cake.

> **Roshan (last line of the chapter):** "Then tomorrow we're going north — and I'm getting my candles back."

She goes up to the castle bed. The lullaby chimes come down, the "z"s float up, and the screen fades to indigo.

**Day 2 ends the way Day 1 ended: everybody goes to bed.**

---

## 7. WHAT DAY 3 INHERITS

Roshan wakes on Day 3 owing herself five candles and one answer. She knows the Prince's name and his face and his grievance, and she knows he has a father called the **Ember King** whom nobody has ever seen — and the only people who know the way are the old folk in the far north, so north is where the day goes. She takes the racer with her, because Day 2 spent the whole chapter teaching a four-year-old that the racer is *how you get somewhere*, and now getting somewhere is the entire point. She takes the twelve imps, who are hers now and know the fire mountain from the inside, and she takes the burnt invitation as the one piece of evidence she has. And she carries forward the thing nobody said out loud at the party: **the boy who took her candles is a boy who was never invited to anything**, which means Chapter 3 is not a hunt. It's a journey to find out who did that to him — and five ember lanterns are waiting at the end of it, one for each candle.

### Continuity notes for the build (not part of the read-aloud)

- **Twelve** is the guest count fixed by Act One and referenced by the chef (cake cut twelve ways), candy maker (twelve bags **plus extras**), farmer (twelve helpings), painter (a hall dressed for twelve), nursery nurse (how many little ones). The extras are the invitation payoff and must not be explained.
- **Two plants in the cold open and the library** — the wordless Prince at the window, and the returned invitation with the burnt corner — are the only two things that make the crash feel prepared rather than sudden. Neither is ever remarked on until the final scene.
- **Order-independence:** only the astronaut is sequenced. Every other callback line (Sparkle's extra bags, Pudding at the table, Lamba's mend not showing, the microphone reaching the back) fires only if that piece is already on the table; a neutral variant plays otherwise.
- **The crate table** appears three times with escalating quality — chalk and sand (craft room), real ribbon and a real bag (playroom/bath), a nearly-convincing spread (backstage) — and is never taken away from them. It is abandoned, not confiscated.
- **Never seen in Chapter 2:** the Ember King. Named four times, seen zero.
- **Sacred audio honoured:** Daddy and Chuck appear using existing recordings only; Sparkle chirps, Mewsha meows, Flower Friend is silent.
- **Cut and staying cut:** Curtain Dragon, Shadow Phantom, Midnight Maestro. No host announces the party — Roshan calls it herself, the hall is the place, and the lights are simply on.

**There is no `rival_nursery`.** The nursery is the cooperative act, and its rival slot is already occupied by Faron — `scripts/opera_career_world_2d.gd:378-381`:

### C2 — THE ASTRONAUT ACT IS RE-ORDERED AGAINST BOTH THE SHIPPED DATA AND §20 *(hard break)*

Draft:
> "…and then the five beats of the launch… She routes the **bubble tubes**… She **patches** the leaks… She turns the **valve**… She builds the **boost**… **LAUNCH.**"
> "**The steal.** In the quiet afterwards, the astronaut imp does the thing he was always going to do."

The shipped astronaut act has **seven** phases, not five, and the steal is phase 5 of 7 — *before* boost and launch (`opera_career_world_2d.gd:117-125`):

```
IMPS!  →  PIPES  →  PATCH  →  VALVE  →  ROCKET CHASE  →  BOOST  →  LAUNCH
```

with `ROCKET CHASE` carrying `"combat": {"count": 8, "captain": true}` and the line *"The imp captain scooped up the little rocket and pressed the silly button! Bop the crew!"*

That is §20's act shape already implemented in data. The draft correctly restores the opening scuffle (`IMPS!`, though it says *"Four plain mischief imps"* where the data ships `"count": 5`), then puts the LAUNCH — the chapter's emotional peak, twelve named bubbles going out — at position 5, and bolts a steal on afterwards. That orphans `ROCKET CHASE` and moves the act's climax off the finale. §14 is the source of the error (it lists only *"PIPES … PATCH … VALVE … BOOST … LAUNCH"* and omits both bop phases), but §20 is the later ruling and it wins.

Draft:
> **Roshan:** "...You weren't stealing it. You were *practising*."

Then, one section later:
> "Nobody explains what just happened. Nobody says the word *sharing*."

She just explained it. §18: *"That single image explains the whole chapter without a word of exposition."* §19: *"It makes the ending inevitable rather than moralising."* The chalk drawing, twelve caught imps holding her things, and Roshan leaving the crate table untouched already carry it; the line converts an enacted lesson into a narrated one, and it is the only line in the chapter that tells the child what to think. ## 1. COMPREHENSION — where she loses the thread

**A. "TWELVE" is the biggest risk in the chapter.**
The guest count is the designed interlock and it is invisible to the audience. Four-year-olds do not have a stable count list to twelve, cannot subitize above ~4, and cannot hold an abstract quantity across eleven acts. Worse, the arithmetic doesn't survive contact: she names twelve friends, then hands Mewsha a thirteenth invitation ("this one I'm giving you myself"), and at the party the actual bodies in the room are twelve friends + Mewsha + Lamba + Pudding + twelve imps. A child who *is* counting will find the number wrong; a child who isn't will never have had it.

**Fix — change the unit from a number to a one-to-one match.** Keep twelve as the build-side constant, but child-facing it is always *"one for everybody, and some spare."* Represent it as a visible set that never changes shape: twelve cards on a board, twelve bags in a row, twelve chairs. Cut the finger-counting recital (twelve names in a row is a memory task with no reward) and let the LAUNCH be the count — she names each friend as their bubble goes. The Sparkle payoff then reads visually: at the doorway there are bags left over, and she doesn't have to go and get more. That is legible. "Twelve" is not.

**B. The cold-open window boy will not register.** Three seconds, wordless, backlit, no sting, and Roshan doesn't see him. The draft's own note — "Roshan doesn't see it. The child watching does" — is optimistic. As staged, a four-year-old sees a dark shape and moves on. This is the entire setup for the cliffhanger, so this is the most consequential comprehension gap in the draft. (Full fix in §5.)

**C. Adult irony that lands only on the reading parent.** Flag these as animator/build notes, not read-aloud text:
- *"he is doing the thing children do when they are looking at something they have never been given"*
- *"the road not taken"* (the boxed shop cake)
- *"an expression the child will recognise instantly, because it is the face you make at somebody else's presents"*
- *"not unkindly, which is worse"*
None of these are damaging — they're good direction. They just must not be spoken.

**D. The boxed shop cake needs one line or it should go.** A perfect uneaten cake sitting on the cart all act is a gun that never fires, and the child's honest question is *"why doesn't she just take that one?"* — which is the theme. Answer it out loud, once: **Kareem: "You could just have that one." / Roshan: "I want to make mine."** One exchange makes the prop the best thesis statement in the chapter. Without it, cut the prop.

**E. "Nobody we know is that warm" is opaque.** "Warm" as a euphemism for "made of fire" is an inference. The *handprint* is the readable object — lean entirely on it. **Huluu: "Look — it's burnt." / Roshan: "Who's got hot hands?"**

**F. The burnt invitation goes into a pocket for forty minutes and comes out as a new object.** Four-year-olds don't carry open mysteries. Give it **one silent re-look** in the middle — best placed immediately after the courtyard ember imps, when the smell matches. Better still: have an ember imp leave a **sooty handprint on the flagstone that matches the one on the invitation.** Matching shapes is a thing she can do; "deduction" is not. That single prop makes the entire detective plot child-visible.

**G. What works and should not be touched:** the two imp species are correctly disambiguated by a *character naming the difference out loud* ("These are not the purple ones" / "...Those aren't ours"). That is exactly the right move and the only one that works at this age. Likewise the Captain's "you'll have to bop ME twice" as a stated rule of the game.

## 2. FEAR CHECK

**FLAG 1 — the window boy is staged in horror grammar.** Backlit silhouette, watching an unaware child, vanishes, leaves a trace. The draft removes the sting and the music but keeps the *shape*. Front-light him. Show his face. Let him be visibly a kid with an ordinary expression. Give him one small human sound — a sniff, shoes scuffing the sill — because silence is what makes him a ghost. Best structural fix: **Mewsha sees him.** Roshan doesn't. The child follows the cat's eyes. Animal-mediated looking removes the surveillance quality entirely and keeps Roshan unaware.

**FLAG 2 — "the hall smells of smoke now."** Smoke indoors is a live anxiety hook for any four-year-old who has had a fire-safety talk. Change it to reuse the cold open: **"the hall smells like a blown-out candle."** Same image, warm, birthday-coded, and it makes the plant do double duty for free.

**FLAG 3 — the dark interval at the crash is the one thing to cut.** *"each one that goes out, he takes, until the five candles are in his fist and the hall is dark except for him"* — that makes him the sole light source, which is monster framing, and the draft then contradicts itself ("the lamps come back on"). **Keep the lamps up throughout.** The loss must read as *"my candles are gone,"* not *"the lights went out."* This is a one-clause deletion and it removes the single most frightening image in the chapter.

**FLAG 4 — the dread gap before anyone speaks.** The light bends, the singing stops, and then a full paragraph of description before a line. Read aloud that's ~15 seconds of held breath. Move **"That's... that's our Prince"** up into the silence, within a beat of the light change, and have the singing trail off because everyone *turns to look* (curiosity), not because they feel it on their faces (dread).

**FLAG 5 — "My father doesn't do birthdays" is the heaviest line in the chapter.** It's the engine and it should stay, but a *sad* child villain is harder for a four-year-old than a *bratty* one, and the settled terms want bratty. Play it bratty on screen — "My father doesn't do birthdays. So I'll take yours." Let the sadness live entirely in the chalk drawing (which she has already seen) and in the Captain's small voice. Evidence can be sad; the boy on screen should not be.

**FLAG 6 — the ten sooty imps outside the window during the party.** Right after "Imps — you're invited," there are ten little guys sitting in the dark who weren't. That quietly contradicts the chapter's own lesson at its warmest moment, and a four-year-old will notice the ones outside. Cheapest fix: someone passes bags and water out the window to them. They still don't come in (they're the King's, not the Prince's), but nobody is excluded on screen.

**Clean, no action needed:** the bops (they scatter giggling, one waves), the ember-imp fights (kettle-hissing is perfectly domestic), and the water. Nothing in the draft has heat ever *hurt* anyone — keep that absolute; no character should ever recoil or say "ow."

## 3. EMOTIONAL BEATS

**The refusal (cold open) reads as a rebuff unless she adds four words.** "No thank you" + "that's the part I *want*" is an abstract preference a four-year-old may hear as Roshan being unkind to Huluu, or as Roshan not getting a party. Add: **"I want to make it. Will you help me?"** That converts a refusal into a shared plan instantly and gives Huluu her whole day's job in one line.

**The invitation lands — conditionally.** It works because of the crate table's three-stage escalation and because the Captain is *holding the paper crown and takes it off*. Both are physical, both are correct. But the first crate sighting is at risk: chalk, sand and pebbles alone read as "a messy table," not "someone else's party." **Put one imp at it, fussing** — straightening the paper tablecloth, placing a pebble carefully. A table is scenery; a table with someone tending it is a story.

**The turn is under-served.** *"...You weren't stealing it. You were practising"* is the hinge, and "practising" is a word she owns and a thing she does — this is the best-chosen word in the draft. But it's currently said to nobody, and then Roshan walks away from twelve frozen children, which to a four-year-old is a non-event or a rejection, not grace. Two fixes: **say the line to them, out loud**, and — at the doorway — **have the imps carry her pieces in and put them on her table themselves.** That's the copy dissolving, unasked, in an action rather than a narrator's aside.

**Break the theft pattern once before the ending needs it broken.** Twelve consecutive "your thing was taken" beats is a lot of fairness-stress, even with same-act resolution. Have the **nursery imp hand the baby back on his own.** He's already characterised as the genuinely gentle one; it costs nothing and it pre-writes the doorway.

**Losing the candles: sad-but-exciting, yes — except for the wish.** Nothing else is touched, everyone is fine, and she has a plan within thirty seconds. That's the right machinery. But she closes her eyes to wish and is *interrupted*: a held breath with no release, and the wish is the part a four-year-old cares about most. Add the reassurance explicitly — **Huluu: "You can still make your wish. We just have to get them back first."** — and let the last line carry it: *"...I'm getting my candles back. And then I'm making my wish."* A cliffhanger at this age must be a **promise she can hold**, not an open wound.

**Is she ever made to feel she failed?** No — and the one moment she is bested (the magician imp nearly doing it) is good and should stay, provided her reaction is **admiring, not stung.** The nursery framing ("the one thing Roshan has never been good at: being quiet") is fine at this age *if she succeeds*; make sure she does.

## 4. PACING — the middle is a slog as written, but the fix is length, not shape

The structural problem is that §2 and §3 are in **different modes**. Act One is dramatized — scenes, quoted lines, beats. The other eleven are *summarized*, and a four-year-old cannot listen to summary. Section 3 is currently ~1,300 words of narrator prose across eleven sub-headings containing **two quoted lines total** (Harper's and Huluu's). Quoted speech is the re-engagement device at this age; the middle has almost none.

Do **not** vary the seven-beat act shape. Identical structure twelve times is correct for four — that's the ritual, and the draft's instinct to repeat Harper's *"Where to next, birthday girl?"* verbatim every time is the best pacing decision in the document. Keep it word-for-word; it's her clock. But cap the racer interstitial at **~10 seconds**. If it becomes a playable race eleven times it is the single thing that kills the chapter.

**Vary the length, not the song. Three tiers:**

| Tier | Jobs | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **FULL ACT** (5) | Astronaut, Chef, Detective, Painter, Magician | These carry the count, the cake, the plot clue, the room-becomes-a-party (biggest visual payoff in the chapter), and the discovery/turn. |
| **HALF ACT** (4) — one job beat, one imp gag, piece lands | Candy Maker, Farmer, Stuffie Surgeon, Nursery | Real emotional content (extras plant, Pudding, Lamba mended, soft corner) but none needs full act architecture. |
| **LIGHT BEAT** (3) — one image, one line, ~20s | Ballerina, Pop Star, Boxer | **Their act is the party itself.** The dance, the microphone reaching the back, and the wild games all pay off in §5 — prepping them at full length spends the payoff twice. |

Every one of the eleven needs **at least one quoted line**, even the light beats. That alone fixes most of the middle's read-aloud problem.

**The two ember encounters:** keep both — they're the only thing making the crash feel prepared — but they are currently the same beat twice. Differentiate by making the second one *produce evidence* (the matching sooty handprint). Then it's not a repeat, it's a clue.

**The crate table's three sightings:** keep all three, unchanged. Cheapest, clearest device in the chapter.

**Compress:** the Prince walking the whole length of the table examining each piece. Lovely, and long. Two pieces, then the cake.

## 5. STRONGEST AND WEAKEST

### Strongest beat: **the water in the courtyard.**
> *"She gives them a cup of water each. They drink it. They stay sitting there for the rest of the day, watching."*

Strongest **line** is the doorway ("...Are we invited?" / "Imps — you're invited") and that's the one she'll repeat — but the water is the beat doing the most work per unit of comprehension, and it's the one most likely to be cut for pacing. It enacts the entire house law in three sentences with zero inference required, it's funny, it's imitable, and it silently pre-writes the ending forty minutes early: *she gives things to people who came to take things.* It costs one prop. **Protect it.**

### Weakest beat: **the cold-open window boy.**
It is simultaneously the least legible moment and the only one staged like a horror shot — and it is the setup the entire cliffhanger rests on. As written, the audience the chapter is for will not encode it, so the crash arrives unprepared for exactly the child it was prepared for.

**The fix, four parts, all cheap:**
1. **Front-light him.** Backlit silhouette = threat; a lit face = a kid.
2. **Mewsha sees him.** Roshan doesn't. The child follows the cat's eyes — the most reliable attention device that exists at this age, and it keeps Roshan unaware without making the moment surveillance.
3. **One small human sound** — a sniff, shoes scuffing the sill. Silence is what makes him a ghost; a boy-noise makes him a boy.
4. **Leave a sooty handprint on the windowsill.** Then the burnt invitation's handprint, then the ember imps' handprint on the flagstone. **Three matching handprints is a chain a four-year-old can genuinely follow** — and it converts the chapter's two "unremarked plants" from an adult device into a shape-matching game she can win. When the Prince appears at the crash, she has already solved it.

That one prop, used three times, fixes the weakest beat, the detective plot's legibility, and the crash's preparedness at the same time.
